In [Language Modeling](/tutorials/llm-fundamentals/language-modeling-and-next-token-prediction/#text-as-a-token-sequence), the story tokenizer used five tokens for “the cat sat”. Two of them were spaces. A tokenizer with entries for “ cat” and “ sat” can write the same sentence in three tokens. Each token takes up one position in the model’s input, so a choice as small as where the spaces go changes how much text fits.

![Three segmentations](/images/llm-fundamentals/15b-three-segmentations.png)

## Text units

A character tokenizer only needs about a hundred symbols to spell ordinary English. It can spell a new name as long as it has the letters. The cost is a longer input. “Unbelievably, tokenizers split words.” takes 37 positions because even a space or a comma gets a position of its own. The number of positions the model processes is its **sequence length**.

A word tokenizer can fit the same sentence into six positions if it has entries for all four words and both punctuation marks. A fixed word list runs into trouble when a story introduces a name like “Okonkwo”. If “Okonkwo”, the typo “recieve” and the code identifier “x_train” are all missing from the list, they all get the same **unknown token**, such as `<unk>`. Their different spellings now look identical to the model.

A **subword** tokenizer can spend one position on a common word and several on a rarer one. In the figure, GPT-2 keeps “ split” and “ words” whole. It spells “Unbelievably” with “Un”, “bel”, “iev” and “ably”. The whole sentence takes ten tokens, and those tokens still preserve its spelling.

That choice can decide whether the [story model](/tutorials/llm-fundamentals/#story-model) sees the end of a story. Its **context window** holds 512 tokens. A 150-word TinyStories story would take about 765 character tokens at the corpus average of 5.1 characters per word, counting spaces. If the model could read only the first 512, it would miss the ending.

The story tokenizer averages about 2.22 tokens per word, as the [vocabulary size](#vocabulary-size) section measures. At that rate, the same story takes about 333 tokens. The model now has room for the whole thing.

Shorter inputs also save [attention](/tutorials/llm-fundamentals/attention-and-positional-information/#quadratic-cost-and-the-key-value-cache) work. When the model reaches the final period in the figure, it has 36 earlier positions to consider with characters and nine with GPT-2 tokens. This reduction applies throughout the sentence because every token adds another position to compare.

## Byte pair encoding

Once tokens can be different sizes, the tokenizer needs a way to choose which pieces to keep. A piece like “th” saves a position every time it replaces “t” followed by “h”, in words such as “the” and “with”. **Byte pair encoding**, or BPE, starts with small pieces. It joins the neighboring pair it sees most often and counts again. Once “th” exists, a later merge can join it with “e” to make “the”.

BPE learns from a body of text called the **training corpus**. **Pre-tokenization** splits that text into chunks. BPE breaks each chunk into small units such as bytes and counts neighboring pairs within each chunk.

A **merge** joins the most frequent pair into one piece. BPE adds that piece to the vocabulary and uses it wherever the pair occurs. Each merge changes which pieces are neighbors, so BPE counts the pairs again before choosing the next one. Training stops when the vocabulary reaches its target size or no pairs remain. The **merge list** records the choices in order.

### The algorithm step by step

Training decides which merges are available. **Encoding** uses that finished list to turn new text into token IDs. In Algorithm 1, the **base symbols** are the pieces training starts with. The small corpus below starts with letters. A byte-level tokenizer starts with bytes.

In round 1, w e appears in “lower”, “lowest”, and “newest”. Their frequencies add to $2 + 3 + 6 = 11$, just ahead of l o and o w at 10. After merging w e, the w in “lower” and “lowest” is already part of we. The next round therefore finds o w only five times, in “low”. This is why pair counts need updating after each merge. The same tie rule picks we s over s t in round 3 and n e over e west in round 5. After seven merges, the vocabulary has its 15 pieces, so training stops.

For new text, there is no need to count which pair is most popular. Algorithm 2 takes the input text $x$ and applies the learned merges in order, using the earliest merge that is available at each step.

A merge only changes words that contain the winning pair. Efficient implementations keep the old counts and update the pairs in those affected words. That saves a full recount after every merge.

A merge saves one token each time it replaces a pair. For two different symbols, a pair with weighted count $c$ therefore saves $c$ tokens. Repeated symbols can overlap. In a a a, the pair a a is counted twice, but only one copy can be merged.

The toy corpus starts with $3 \times 5 + 5 \times 2 + 6 \times 3 + 6 \times 6 = 79$ letters. The seven chosen pair counts are 11, 10, 9, 9, 6, 6, and 5, for a saving of 56 tokens. That leaves $79 - 56 = 23$. Counting the final pieces gives the same total, with 5 for “low”, $3 \times 2$ for “lower”, $2 \times 3$ for “lowest”, and 6 for “newest”. This is a **greedy** choice. Each merge takes the best saving available now, and the earlier choices shape what is possible later. Merging w e first, for example, prevents “low” from forming inside “lower”.

</details>

Real tokenizers often start with the 256 possible byte values. This is **byte-level BPE**, and the [byte level fallback](#byte-level-fallback) section explains how it represents text the tokenizer has never seen. Rules for digits can also change the result. GPT-2 encodes “ the lowest price is 12345” as Ġthe, Ġlowest, Ġprice, Ġis, Ġ123, and 45. SmolLM2 and Llama 2 split every digit into its own token. The training corpus also matters. English-heavy text teaches the tokenizer many English pieces, so it may have to use shorter pieces for other languages or code, as the [fertility](#fertility) section shows.

The story tokenizer learns 3,836 merges from 20,000 TinyStories stories, starting with 256 byte values. Its first five new pieces are “he”, “an”, “the”, “ed”, and “and”. The third merge can make “the” because the first has already made “he”. Later merges complete words such as “thermometer”, “fireplace”, and “sailing”. A word such as “photosynthesis” can still be written with familiar pieces. The tokenizer uses photo, sy, n, thes, and is.

## WordPiece and Unigram tokenizers

In the small corpus from the [byte pair encoding](#byte-pair-encoding) section, BPE joined w and e first because they appeared together 11 times. Those letters are also common on their own. Meanwhile s and t meet just nine times, but every s is followed by t. A tokenizer can use that information to notice pairs that belong together, even when their raw count is lower.

**WordPiece** also builds larger pieces from smaller ones. A common way to explain its scoring rule is to divide the pair count by how often each piece appears on its own. The division gives a pair credit for occurring together more often than the individual counts would suggest. BERT ([Devlin et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1810.04805)) uses WordPiece with a vocabulary of about 30,000.

$$
\text{score}(a, b) = \frac{c(a\,b)}{c(a)\,c(b)}
$$

In the formula, $c(a\,b)$ counts the pair and $c(a)$ and $c(b)$ count the pieces separately. The corpus has counts $c(\text{s}) = 9$, $c(\text{t}) = 9$, $c(\text{w}) = 16$, and $c(\text{e}) = 17$. The pair s t scores $9/(9 \times 9) = 0.111$. The more frequent w e pair scores $11/(16 \times 17) = 0.040$. Its larger numerator is outweighed by how common both letters are.

The same example shows what “more often than chance” means. The 79 letters make 63 neighboring pairs. If each side of a pair were drawn independently in proportion to the letter counts, s t would appear about $63 \times (9/79) \times (9/79) \approx 0.8$ times. Its nine actual occurrences are about 11 times that estimate. The pair w e occurs 11 times against about 2.7 expected, or about four times as often. The ratio of actual to expected count is the WordPiece score times $79^2/63$. That constant is the same for every pair, so it leaves the ranking the same.

This is the likelihood intuition. A simple model that treats the letters independently badly underestimates how often s and t occur together. Giving that pair its own piece can raise the probability the simple model assigns to the training text. WordPiece also marks pieces that continue a word with `##`, so “tokenization” can split into `token` and `##ization`.

A **Unigram tokenizer** ([Kudo](https://arxiv.org/abs/1804.10959)) starts with a large list of candidate pieces. It assigns each one a probability $p(\cdot)$ and scores each possible split by multiplying the probabilities of its pieces. This works like the sentence probability in [Language Modeling](/tutorials/llm-fundamentals/language-modeling-and-next-token-prediction/#the-chain-rule-of-probability), with the simpler assumption here that each piece has a fixed probability.

For “lowest”, suppose $p(\text{low}) = 0.10$, $p(\text{est}) = 0.05$, $p(\text{lo}) = 0.08$, and $p(\text{west}) = 0.04$. Give each single letter probability 0.02. Then low + est scores $0.10 \times 0.05 = 0.005$, lo + west scores $0.08 \times 0.04 = 0.0032$, and six single letters score $0.02^6 \approx 6 \times 10^{-11}$. The tokenizer picks low + est because it has the highest score. Training re-estimates the probabilities and removes pieces whose removal hurts the corpus probability least. This removal is called **pruning**.

BPE and Unigram decide which pieces to keep. **SentencePiece** ([Kudo and Richardson](https://arxiv.org/abs/1808.06226)) is a library that can run either method on raw text. It treats the space as a symbol shown as ▁. For example, “Hello world” can become ▁Hello and ▁world. This approach works for text without spaces between words, such as Chinese, Japanese, and Thai. Llama 2 uses a 32,000-entry SentencePiece BPE tokenizer ([Touvron et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.09288)).

## Byte level fallback

A tokenizer can learn useful pieces from millions of words and still encounter a character it has never seen. It can fall back to the bytes that store the character. Those bytes come from a fixed set of 256 values, so the tokenizer has a way to spell unfamiliar text.

A **byte** is a number from 0 to 255. **UTF-8** is a rule for storing Unicode text as bytes. A single code point takes one to four bytes. The letter a takes just the byte 61 in hexadecimal. An accented letter such as é takes two bytes, a Devanagari letter or 東 takes three, and 😀 takes four. That is why a byte tokenizer can spend several tokens on one visible letter before any merges help.

UTF-8 uses the first byte to indicate the length, and the following bytes have values from 80 to BF in hexadecimal. That structure lets a decoder put the bytes back together. An individual token may end in the middle of a character, so its bytes can look garbled in isolation and still decode correctly as part of the complete text.

![Utf8 bytes](/images/llm-fundamentals/15b-utf8-bytes.png)

**Byte fallback** reserves one token for each of the 256 possible bytes. If the tokenizer has no piece for 東, it can use three byte tokens to spell it. Llama 2 uses this approach.

**Byte-level BPE** starts with bytes from the beginning and learns merges over them. GPT-2’s tokenizer ([Radford et al.](https://cdn.openai.com/better-language-models/language_models_are_unsupervised_multitask_learners.pdf)) uses five tokens for “ 東京”, which contains one space and two characters stored in seven bytes. Some of the tokens represent only part of a character and display as � on their own. The [story model](/tutorials/llm-fundamentals/#story-model) also uses byte-level BPE, with byte IDs 0 to 255. Its learned merges leave the characters in “ 東京” as separate byte tokens. Counting the space, it uses all seven bytes as tokens.

Both approaches can represent any valid UTF-8 text. If the tokenizer changes the text through normalization before encoding, decoding recovers that normalized spelling and spacing. Being able to represent a character is the first step. The model still has to learn what those pieces mean, and a string that breaks into many rare pieces takes up more of the context window. The [fertility](#fertility) section shows how large that cost can be.

## Vocabulary size

A bigger vocabulary can shorten the same story by keeping more text in one token. Each new token also needs a row of learned numbers for the model to read it. That row is the token’s **embedding**. With $V$ tokens and $d$ numbers per row, the **embedding table** $E$ has $V$ rows of width $d$.

The **output head** $W$ has the same shape. It reads the final hidden vector $\mathbf{h}$ and produces one raw score, or logit $z_j$, for every token $j$. [Softmax](/tutorials/llm-fundamentals/language-modeling-and-next-token-prediction/#logits-and-softmax) turns the scores into next-token probabilities.

The [story model](/tutorials/llm-fundamentals/#story-model) shares one table between the embedding and output head, as the [weight tying](#weight-tying) section explains. Each number in the table is one **parameter**. With 4,096 entries and width 384, that is $4{,}096 \times 384 = 1{,}572{,}864$ parameters, or 14.3% of the model’s 11,015,040. At 16,384 entries, the table would hold $16{,}384 \times 384 = 6{,}291{,}456$ parameters. The rest of the model stays the same. The total would rise by 4,718,592 to 15,733,632, with about 40% in the token table.

A bigger vocabulary can save work by giving the model fewer tokens, but each remaining token becomes more expensive to score. Reading the input embedding takes one row lookup. The output head compares the final vector with every row. Counting a multiplication and an addition for each entry gives $2 \times d \times V$ [floating-point operations (FLOPs)](/tutorials/llm-fundamentals/the-transformer-block-and-model-anatomy/#flops-per-token) per token. That is 3,145,728 FLOPs at 4,096 entries and 12,582,912 at 16,384.

The rest of the story model has 9,442,176 parameters and costs about 18.88 million FLOPs per token in this estimate. Including the output head raises the total from 22.03 million FLOPs per token at 4,096 entries to 31.47 million at 16,384, about 43% more. At about 24,600 entries, the head alone would cost as much arithmetic as the rest of the model.

## Fertility
A tokenizer can use one token for a common word in one language and several for its translation in another. **Fertility** $F$ is the average number of tokens per word. It depends on how much of that language the tokenizer’s training text covered and which pieces it learned.

Consider “The doctor said the test results will be ready next week”. GPT-2 uses 12 tokens for its 11 English words, a fertility of about $12/11 \approx 1.1$. The Russian translation takes 79 tokens, and the Hindi translation takes 94. Relative to the English sentence, those are 6.6 and 7.8 times as many. Even the Spanish and German translations use about 2.4 times as many tokens as English. Sharing an alphabet helps only so much when the tokenizer learned mostly English merges.

Qwen2.5 learned its 151,665 pieces from multilingual text. It uses 20 tokens for the Russian sentence, about a quarter of GPT-2’s count. The Chinese translation takes just 8 tokens because common Chinese strings can be single pieces. Hindi remains the most expensive of the languages shown for Qwen2.5, at 58 tokens or about 4.8 times its English count. A Devanagari letter needs three UTF-8 bytes, which can become three separate tokens if the tokenizer has no suitable merge.

![Fertility across languages](/images/llm-fundamentals/15b-fertility-across-languages.png)

The **token premium** compares the token counts of translations that say the same thing. The English count is the baseline here. [Petrov et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.15425) measured premiums above 10 for some tokenizer and language pairs. The [story model](/tutorials/llm-fundamentals/#story-model) shows how that can happen. Its tokenizer learned from English children’s stories. For the same example, it uses 24 English tokens and 159 Hindi tokens, about 6.6 times as many. The Hindi sentence uses one token per byte. That difference affects how much text fits in the window. At the rates in this example, 512 Hindi tokens hold roughly as much content as 77 English tokens with that tokenizer. The model still does about 22.0 million FLOPs of weight arithmetic per token. Billing per token can show the same effect. If the GPT-2 ratio held for a longer answer of 130 English tokens, the Hindi version would take about 1,020 tokens and cost about 7.8 times as much at the same rate. The figure uses a single sentence. A production estimate needs representative text from each language.

### Tokenization failure modes

A tokenizer can preserve a word even when the model struggles to count its letters. GPT-2 encodes `" strawberry"` as one token. To count the three letters `r`, the model needs to recover the spelling from what it learned about the whole piece. Without the leading space, the tokenizer uses `st`, `raw`, and `berry`. The new split still leaves the counting to the model. A character-aware tool can read the decoded word and count the letters directly.

A single space can change which vector the model reads. GPT-2 assigns ID 9246 to `"cat"` and 3797 to `" cat"`. Both take one position, but each ID looks up a different row and was trained in different contexts. The story tokenizer handles this case differently by giving the space its own token.

The tokenizer and the model can learn from different text. A piece might be frequent when the tokenizer learns its vocabulary and rare in the model’s training data after filtering. An **under-trained token** then gets few chances to learn how it is used, either as an input or as the next-token target. The output softmax can still update its row on examples where another token comes next. Some under-trained entries can trigger unrelated responses or repetition. These are informally called **glitch tokens**. [Fishing for Magikarp](https://aclanthology.org/2024.emnlp-main.649/) documents examples and traces them to gaps between the corpora.

## Special tokens and chat templates

In a chat, the model needs to know who wrote each message and where the reply should start. A user can type the word “assistant” in ordinary text, so a chat template can mark roles with dedicated IDs. **Special tokens** are reserved IDs with jobs such as marking a boundary or a role.

The **beginning of sequence token** (BOS) marks the start. The **end of sequence token** (EOS) marks where the model should stop. Llama 2 writes them as `<s>` and `</s>`. The [story model](/tutorials/llm-fundamentals/#story-model) uses IDs 256 and 257, directly after its 256 byte IDs. Training shows the model EOS at the end of examples so it learns to produce it when a reply is complete.

A **padding token** fills empty positions so shorter sequences can share a batch with longer ones. The loss skips those positions. The story model reuses EOS for unused slots at the end of a packed row and records which slots to skip. Chat models also use **role markers** to label messages from roles such as system, user, and assistant.

A **chat template** is the recipe for laying out messages and control tokens in a single sequence. It specifies the order, the separators such as newlines, and the place where the reply begins. The story model handles one user turn and reserves USER at ID 258 and ASSISTANT at ID 259. Its prompt consists of BOS, USER, the request, and ASSISTANT. The model then writes the answer and can finish it with EOS. “Use these words: kite, duck.” takes 13 tokens on its own and 16 after adding the three prompt markers. “Use” takes two pieces, “U” and “se”. Normal text encoding produces only byte and merge IDs, so it cannot accidentally turn a typed word into one of the reserved IDs 256 to 259. During generation, the notebook allows EOS and blocks BOS, USER, and ASSISTANT. Larger chat models add more structure. One three-message example under Qwen2.5’s ChatML template takes 26 tokens, including three `<|im_start|>` and three `<|im_end|>` markers.

The figure also shows what happens if the same request arrives in Llama 2’s `[INST]` format. The story tokenizer spells out `<s>`, `[INST]`, and `[/INST]` as ordinary text. That takes 31 tokens, nearly twice as many as the expected prompt. More importantly, the story model gets ordinary text pieces in the places where it expects role markers.

![Chat template tokens](/images/llm-fundamentals/15b-chat-template-tokens.png)

The story model learns what USER and ASSISTANT mean during **fine-tuning** on request and answer pairs (see [Post-Training](/tutorials/llm-fundamentals/post-training-from-base-model-to-assistant/)). Its pretraining stories use BOS, story tokens, and EOS. The fine-tuned model expects the markers from those later examples to tell it when to start the answer. A different layout can still tokenize cleanly and cause the model to continue the user’s turn or keep generating past the expected end. Even a missing newline, a repeated BOS, or a mismatch between the training and serving templates can change the sequence the model sees.

## Embedding lookup

Once the tokenizer has chosen the IDs, the first layer needs a vector for each position. **Embedding lookup** uses the token ID $i$ to pick a row from the embedding table $E$. The row $\mathbf{e}_i$ contains the $d$ numbers the model will read. The [notebook check](/tutorials/llm-fundamentals/notebook/#lookup) uses the first token of “fox”. It gets the same 384 numbers by reading the row directly and by selecting it with a one-hot vector. The practice example below shows how both methods pick the same row.

A token ID is a label. In the [byte pair encoding](#byte-pair-encoding) section’s toy vocabulary, “we” has ID 8, “lo” has 9, and “low” has 14. Treating the IDs as numbers would make “we” and “lo” look unusually close. In the lookup table, every token has its own row. Training can move the vectors for “lo” and “low” closer if that helps predictions, even though their IDs are five apart. Tokenizers such as GPT-2 number bytes first and then add IDs in the order of the learned merges.

Multiplication by a **one-hot vector** gives the same result as a lookup. The vector $\mathbf{o}_i$ has $V$ entries. It puts 1 at position $i$ and 0 everywhere else. In $\mathbf{o}_i E = \mathbf{e}_i$, multiplying by zero drops every row except $i$, and multiplying by one keeps the selected row exactly.

A row lookup is a direct memory read. The [story model](/tutorials/llm-fundamentals/#story-model) stores 4,096 rows of 384 learned numbers, for the 1,572,864 parameters counted in the [vocabulary size](#vocabulary-size) section. At four bytes per number in float32, the table takes about 6.3 MB. One lookup reads 384 numbers, or 1,536 bytes. A full 512-token context reads at most 512 distinct rows. The model stacks the selected rows into a $512 \times 384$ matrix with 196,608 numbers, about 0.79 MB, and sends it to the first layer.

The lookup tells us which rows get a training signal from the input. A parameter’s **gradient** measures how the loss changes when that parameter increases. Training moves against the gradient. If a token never appears in a batch, the lookups in that batch never use its row, so they give it zero gradient. In one 512-token input, at least 3,584 of the 4,096 rows receive no signal from lookups.

The story model also uses the table to score outputs, as the [weight tying](#weight-tying) section explains. The softmax can therefore give the same row a gradient when other tokens appear as targets. At the input, each occurrence of a token still starts from the same embedding row. [Attention](/tutorials/llm-fundamentals/attention-and-positional-information/) shows how the model can use position and surrounding text to make those occurrences behave differently.

## Embedding geometry

The vectors learned for “cat” and “dog” can point much more nearly in the same direction than those for “cat” and “car”. To describe that difference, we need a way to compare directions even when one vector is longer than the other.

The **dot product** of vectors $\mathbf{u}$ and $\mathbf{v}$ multiplies matching coordinates and adds them. If two vectors point in similar directions, their dot product can be large. Making either vector longer can also increase it. **Cosine similarity** divides by both lengths to measure just the angle $\theta$ between them.

$$
\cos\theta = \frac{\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v}}{\lVert \mathbf{u} \rVert \, \lVert \mathbf{v} \rVert}
$$

In the formula, $\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v}$ is the dot product and $\lVert \mathbf{u} \rVert$ is the length of $\mathbf{u}$, found by taking the square root of the sum of its squared coordinates. A cosine of 1 means the directions agree, 0 means the vectors are perpendicular, and −1 means they point in opposite directions.

The figure uses three two-number embeddings. They are cat (3, 1), dog (2, 2), and car (−1, 3). Cat and dog have dot product $3 \times 2 + 1 \times 2 = 8$. Their lengths are $\sqrt{10} \approx 3.162$ and $\sqrt{8} \approx 2.828$, so their cosine is $8 / 8.944 \approx 0.894$. That is an angle of about 27 degrees. Cat and car have dot product $3 \times (-1) + 1 \times 3 = 0$, so they are perpendicular.

Stretching cat to (6, 2) doubles its dot product with dog to 16 and doubles its length. The two changes cancel in the cosine, which stays at 0.894. That is why cosine works for searches that care about direction even when the vectors have different lengths.

![Cosine similarity](/images/llm-fundamentals/15b-cosine-similarity.png)

The language model’s training can produce these similarities. The score for token $j$ is $z_j = \mathbf{h} \cdot \mathbf{w}_j$, where $\mathbf{h}$ is the final vector at this position and $\mathbf{w}_j$ is row $j$ of the output head $W$. If “cat” and “dog” often work as continuations of similar prefixes, training can move both output rows toward similar hidden vectors. Input embeddings can also become similar when the tokens appear in contexts that give them similar gradients.

Real embedding tables can be **anisotropic**. Many rows share a common direction, so even unrelated tokens might have a positive cosine such as 0.3. That is why a similarity value needs a baseline from the same model. The input rows here represent individual tokens. [Vector search](/tutorials/llm-fundamentals/using-llms-in-applications/#embedding-models-and-vector-search) uses models that build embeddings for whole passages or questions.

## Weight tying

The input table turns “cat” into a vector. The output head goes the other way. It scores “cat” and every other token against the final vector. Both need one row of $d$ numbers for each of the $V$ tokens, so one table can do both jobs.

**Weight tying** means learning one shared table. The output head is the embedding table, so $W = E$. To score token $j$, the model takes the dot product $z_j = \mathbf{h} \cdot \mathbf{e}_j$ with that token’s own embedding. The same row then learns from times the token appears in the input and from the loss on the output scores. [Press and Wolf](https://arxiv.org/abs/1608.05859) found that this can also improve language modeling.

The [story model](/tutorials/llm-fundamentals/#story-model) saves 1,572,864 parameters by tying the tables. It would have 12,587,904 parameters with separate tables, so sharing removes 12.5% of that total and saves about 6.3 MB of float32 weights. The output head still scores all the rows, so the forward arithmetic at the head stays the same.

The saving is most noticeable when the vocabulary is large relative to the rest of the model. At width 2,048 and vocabulary size 128,000, one table takes 262,144,000 parameters, about a quarter of a 1B model. GPT-2, Llama 3.2 1B, and the Gemma family tie input and output weights. Llama 2 7B keeps its two $32{,}000 \times 4{,}096$ tables separate. Sharing would save about 1.9% of the 6.74B parameters, and separate tables can specialize for reading and scoring. [Parameter Efficient Adaptation](/tutorials/llm-deep-dive/parameter-efficient-adaptation/#sharing-levels) covers other ways of sharing weights.

At this point one position holds one embedding vector. The [Attention chapter](/tutorials/llm-fundamentals/attention-and-positional-information/#token-mixing) starts from those vectors and shows how they pass information between positions.
