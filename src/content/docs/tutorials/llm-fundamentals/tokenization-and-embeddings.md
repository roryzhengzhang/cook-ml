"Unbelievably" could be one piece of text, or three, or twelve. Every language model has to settle questions like that before it reads anything, because it can only predict entries from its fixed vocabulary, and the endless variety of real text has to be cut to fit that list. Cutting text into tokens and turning each token into numbers is the first thing that happens to any text a model sees. The choices made there decide how long every document is, what each language costs, and which strings a model can never look inside.

Two separate decisions hide inside "turn text into numbers", and people often blur them. The **tokenizer** decides where the cuts go, which fixes how many tokens a document costs and which strings the model can ever see as one unit. The **embedding table** then gives each token id one row of learned numbers. That row is called the token's **embedding**. These rows are what the rest of the network actually computes with. The figure cuts one sentence three ways to show what the first decision trades.

![Three segmentations](/images/llm-fundamentals/15b-three-segmentations.png)

The subword cut keeps most of the length saving of whole words without their failure. The sentence costs 10 tokens instead of 37, so attention compares 100 pairs of positions instead of 1,369. Yet a rare word that a word list would miss is still spelled from pieces the tokenizer has, as "Unbelievably" is from four, so nothing turns into an unknown token. The cost moves into the vocabulary. GPT-2 needs 50,257 entries where characters needed about a hundred, and every entry adds a row to the tables that turn tokens into vectors and score the next token. Almost every design question in this chapter is about where to stand between those two costs, and about which strings a model can no longer see inside once they are fused into one piece.

Those two decisions split this chapter, though not evenly. Most of it is about where the cuts go. Weighing characters, words and subword pieces settles what a good cut has to achieve. Byte pair encoding then shows how a vocabulary of pieces is learned from counts, one merge at a time, and WordPiece and Unigram show what changes when pieces are scored by probability instead. Byte-level fallback closes the last gap, since a learned vocabulary can still meet a character it has never seen. Only once a tokenizer exists can its price be measured, in the size of its tables, the tokens each language pays per word, and the special tokens that frame a chat. The shorter second part turns token ids into vectors. The lookup comes first because an id carries no meaning by itself. The geometry of the learned vectors shows what training puts into them, and weight tying asks whether the same table can also score the next token.

## Text units
Before any learning happens, someone has to decide what counts as one unit of text, and that choice trades one cost against another. One is the **sequence length** $n$, the number of tokens a text becomes. The other is the size of the vocabulary, the list of pieces the model must know. Making the pieces bigger shortens the sequence but lengthens the list, and making them smaller does the reverse.

Characters need only about a hundred symbols for English, so nothing is ever missing. But the opening sentence "Unbelievably, tokenizers split words." becomes 37 tokens. Length is expensive because of **attention**, the step in which every position compares itself with every earlier position. [Attention](/tutorials/llm-fundamentals/attention-and-positional-information/) develops it. Its work grows with $n^2$, so 37 tokens mean about 1,369 position pairs where 10 tokens mean 100. Length also eats the **context window**, the maximum number of tokens the model can read at once. In the [story model](/tutorials/llm-fundamentals/#story-model) it is 512 tokens. TinyStories text averages about 5.1 characters per word including the space, so 512 characters hold about 100 words.

Splitting on whole words turns the same sentence into just 6 tokens, far shorter than 37. The problem is that no list of words is ever complete. English alone has hundreds of thousands of word forms, and real text also contains names, typos, numbers and code identifiers, so words outside any fixed list keep appearing. A word-level model has to replace each of them with one shared placeholder, the **unknown token**, usually written `<unk>`. After that replacement, a name such as "Okonkwo", a typo such as "recieve", and a code name such as "x_train" all reach the model as the same id, so it cannot tell them apart or learn anything about any of them.

A **subword** vocabulary keeps frequent words whole and builds rare ones from smaller pieces. GPT-2's 50,257-piece tokenizer keeps " split" and " words" whole and builds "Unbelievably" from "Un", "bel", "iev" and "ably", for 10 tokens in total. The story model's 4,096-piece tokenizer averages 2.22 tokens per word on TinyStories, as the [vocabulary size](#vocabulary-size) section measures. That is more than most subword tokenizers need, because it keeps every space as a token of its own (the [byte pair encoding](#byte-pair-encoding) section). Its 512-token context still holds about 230 words instead of 100, so an average story of about 340 tokens fits whole. Nearly every widely used large language model, or LLM, uses some form of subword tokenizer, and models that read raw bytes directly remain mostly research systems.

## Byte pair encoding

The most common way to choose subword pieces is to let frequency decide. If "th" shows up constantly in English, it is worth giving it its own symbol, and once "th" exists, "the" may become worth a symbol too. **Byte pair encoding**, abbreviated BPE, applies that idea greedily, and [Sennrich et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1508.07909) brought it from data compression to machine translation in 2016.

The name comes from that compression origin. Philip Gage's 1994 method shrank a file by finding the pair of adjacent bytes that occurred most often, replacing every copy with one unused byte, and repeating. A tokenizer uses the same loop for a different goal. Each new symbol it creates shortens every text that contains the pair, so the pieces it ends up with are the ones that save the most tokens on typical text.

Training a BPE tokenizer starts with every word split into single characters. It then counts every adjacent pair of symbols in the **training corpus**, the sample of text the tokenizer learns from. Each pair is weighted by how often its word appears. The most frequent pair becomes one new symbol through a **merge**, every occurrence in the corpus is rewritten, and the counting repeats until the vocabulary reaches the target size $V$. The output is the **merge list**, the ordered sequence of merges. That list together with the base characters is the whole tokenizer. Before any counting, the text is cut into words by a fixed rule, a step called **pre-tokenization**. Merges are only allowed inside these words, so a token never spans two words and the tokenizer does not waste entries on frequent phrases such as "of the". Spaces are kept rather than thrown away, because the model has to reproduce them when it writes text. GPT-2's tokenizer attaches each space to the word after it and prints it as Ġ, so " the" and "the" are different tokens. SentencePiece is a tokenizer library used by Llama 1 and 2, and it writes the space as ▁. The [story model](/tutorials/llm-fundamentals/#story-model)'s tokenizer keeps each space as a piece of its own. Its pre-tokenizer cuts text into runs of spaces, runs of letters and digits, and runs of other symbols, so "the cat sat" becomes "the", a space, "cat", a space and "sat" before any merge. A single space is always token 32, the byte value of a space, and "cat" is token 616 whether or not a space comes before it. Counting each distinct word once and multiplying by its frequency is also what makes training fast enough, since a large corpus has billions of words but far fewer distinct ones.

### The algorithm step by step

Written as a procedure, BPE has two parts. Training reads a corpus once and learns the merge list. **Encoding** is the second part. It uses the finished tokenizer's fixed list to turn any new text into token ids. Algorithm 1 is the training part. Its **base symbols** are the pieces every word starts as. They are characters in the example below and bytes in real tokenizers. A pair count adds up how often two symbols sit side by side, and a word that appears five times in the corpus counts five times.

The pair o w counted 10 in round 1 but only 5 in round 2, because merge 1 used up the w inside lower and lowest. After round 7 the vocabulary has 15 symbols, so line 3 stops the loop and the merge list holds seven pairs.

Real implementations of training do not recount every pair after each merge. They keep the pair counts and update only the words that contained the merged pair, which is what lets a tokenizer with tens of thousands of merges train on billions of words.

The run also shows why merging the most frequent pair is the right greedy choice. A merge with weighted count $c$ turns $c$ pairs of symbols into $c$ single symbols, so it shortens the corpus by exactly $c$ tokens, as long as the pair joins two different symbols (in a run such as a a a, the pair a a is counted twice but merged once). The corpus starts as $3 \times 5 + 5 \times 2 + 6 \times 3 + 6 \times 6 = 79$ characters. The seven merges have counts 11, 10, 9, 9, 6, 6 and 5, which add up to 56, so the corpus ends at $79 - 56 = 23$ tokens. Counting the final pieces word by word gives the same total, with 5 for low, $3 \times 2$ for lower, $2 \times 3$ for lowest and 6 for newest. Picking the largest count at each step gives the biggest saving available at that step. It does not guarantee the best possible vocabulary overall, since an early merge can block a later one, the way "we" stopped "low" from forming inside "lower".

Real tokenizers change three details of this toy version. First, the base symbols are usually the 256 possible byte values rather than letters. This is called **byte-level BPE**, and it means any string at all can be encoded, as the [byte level fallback](#byte-level-fallback) section explains. Second, many tokenizers add rules for digits. On " the lowest price is 12345", GPT-2's tokenizer gives Ġthe, Ġlowest, Ġprice, Ġis, Ġ123 and 45. It splits the number wherever its merges happen to stop. The SmolLM2 and Llama 2 tokenizers split every digit into its own token, so numbers are always cut the same way. Third, the training corpus decides everything. A tokenizer trained mostly on English text learns long English pieces and cuts other languages and code into many short ones, which is the cost the [fertility](#fertility) section measures.

The story model's tokenizer shows these choices on a real corpus. It starts from the 256 byte values and learns 3,836 merges from 20,000 TinyStories stories. Its first merges are "he", "an", "the", "ed" and "and", so "the" becomes one token at the third merge, built from "t" and the "he" made just before. Late merges complete longer words that the stories use often enough, such as "thermometer", "fireplace" and "sailing". A word the stories never use falls apart wherever its frequent fragments are. "Photosynthesis" becomes photo, sy, n, thes and is, five tokens for one word.

## WordPiece and Unigram tokenizers

Byte pair encoding merges whichever pair appears most often, but a frequent pair is not always one that belongs together. The corpus from the [byte pair encoding](#byte-pair-encoding) section shows the problem. BPE merged w and e first because the pair occurs 11 times. Yet w and e are also the two most common letters in that corpus, so they meet often simply because each one is everywhere. The letter w occurs 16 times and e occurs 17 times. The pair s t occurs only 9 times, but every s in the corpus is followed by t, which makes s t the tighter unit. WordPiece and Unigram are two other tokenizer families, and they score candidate pieces with probabilities instead of raw counts.

**WordPiece** keeps the merging of small pieces from byte pair encoding (BPE) into larger ones, but it scores a pair by how much more often it occurs than chance would predict. BERT ([Devlin et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1810.04805)) uses it with a vocabulary of about 30,000.

$$
\text{score}(a, b) = \frac{c(a\,b)}{c(a)\,c(b)}
$$

Here $c(a\,b)$ is the count of the pair and $c(a)$ and $c(b)$ are the counts of each symbol on its own. Choosing the highest score approximately maximizes the **likelihood** of the corpus, meaning the probability a simple model built from the pieces assigns to the whole training text. On the corpus from the [byte pair encoding](#byte-pair-encoding) section the letter counts are $c(\text{s}) = 9$, $c(\text{t}) = 9$, $c(\text{w}) = 16$ and $c(\text{e}) = 17$. The pair s t scores $9/(9 \times 9) = 0.111$ because s is never seen without t. That is the highest score of any pair. The pair w e that BPE merged first scores only $11/(16 \times 17) = 0.040$, since both letters are common everywhere.

The phrase "more often than chance" can be made exact on the same corpus. Its 79 letters form 63 neighbouring pairs, and s and t each make up 9 of the 79 letters. If the letters were shuffled at random, s followed by t would turn up about $63 \times (9/79) \times (9/79) \approx 0.8$ times, yet the corpus has it 9 times, about 11 times the chance count. The pair w e would turn up about 2.7 times by chance and actually appears 11 times, only 4 times the chance count. The ratio of actual count to chance count is the WordPiece score multiplied by $79^2/63$. That factor is the same for every pair, so ranking pairs by the score ranks them by how far they beat chance. This is also the link to likelihood. A simple model that treats every piece as independent expects s t less than once and so underrates it most, and giving that pair its own piece removes the largest error of that kind. WordPiece marks a piece that continues a word with `##`, so a WordPiece tokenizer splits "tokenization" into token and ##ization.

A **Unigram tokenizer** ([Kudo](https://arxiv.org/abs/1804.10959)) works in the opposite direction. It starts from a large candidate vocabulary, gives each piece a probability $p(\cdot)$, and segments a word into the pieces whose probabilities multiply to the largest value. In a small example, $p(\text{low}) = 0.10$, $p(\text{est}) = 0.05$, $p(\text{lo}) = 0.08$ and $p(\text{west}) = 0.04$, and every single letter has probability 0.02. With those values, low + est scores $0.10 \times 0.05 = 0.005$, lo + west scores $0.08 \times 0.04 = 0.0032$, and six single letters score $0.02^6 \approx 6 \times 10^{-11}$, so the tokenizer picks low + est. Training alternates between re-estimating these probabilities and **pruning** until the vocabulary shrinks to the target size. Pruning removes the pieces whose loss would lower the corpus likelihood least.

**SentencePiece** ([Kudo and Richardson](https://arxiv.org/abs/1808.06226)) is a library that runs BPE or Unigram directly on raw text, treating the space as an ordinary symbol written ▁. "Hello world" becomes ▁Hello and ▁world, so decoding is exact and the tokenizer needs no rule about where words begin. That matters for Chinese, Japanese and Thai, which are written without spaces. Llama 2's 32,000 entry tokenizer is SentencePiece BPE ([Touvron et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.09288)).

## Byte level fallback

Even a large subword vocabulary will meet a character it has never seen, such as a rare Chinese character, an emoji, or a symbol from a math paper. A tokenizer needs some way to write such characters down without giving up and emitting an unknown token. The answer is to fall back to the raw bytes the computer already uses to store the text.

A **byte** is a number from 0 to 255, so there are exactly 256 of them. **UTF-8** is the standard rule for storing any character as one to four bytes, and the count depends on the script. The letter a is the single byte 61 in hexadecimal. Accented Latin letters such as é and Cyrillic letters take 2 bytes, the Devanagari letters used for Hindi and Chinese characters such as 東 take 3, and the emoji 😀 takes 4. Text in any script is therefore a sequence of those 256 values, but not an equally long one. A Hindi sentence already has about three bytes for every character before any tokenizer runs, so a tokenizer built on bytes needs merges just to bring it back to one token per character. UTF-8 also marks which bytes belong together. The first byte of a character says how many follow, and every byte after it lies between 80 and BF. Such a continuation byte means nothing on its own, which is why a tokenizer that cuts a character between tokens produces pieces that cannot be printed by themselves.

![Utf8 bytes](/images/llm-fundamentals/15b-utf8-bytes.png)

Two designs use this. **Byte fallback** keeps the 256 byte values as reserved vocabulary entries and spells any character that has no piece as its UTF-8 bytes, so a missing 東 costs 3 tokens instead of one unknown token. Llama 2 uses this design. **Byte level BPE** goes further and runs byte pair encoding on bytes from the start, so the 256 bytes are the base alphabet and every merge combines bytes. It was introduced with GPT-2 ([Radford et al.](https://cdn.openai.com/better-language-models/language_models_are_unsupervised_multitask_learners.pdf)). The string " 東京" is a space and two characters stored in 7 bytes. GPT-2's tokenizer turns it into 5 tokens, several of which are fragments of a character that print as � on their own. The [story model](/tutorials/llm-fundamentals/#story-model)'s tokenizer is byte-level BPE as well, with IDs 0 to 255 for the bytes. Its training stories contain no Chinese, so it has no merge for these bytes and spends 7 tokens on " 東京", one for the space and one for each byte.

Both designs can represent any valid UTF-8 byte sequence. An exact round trip also depends on preprocessing. A tokenizer that normalizes the text can recover the normalized form, while the original spelling or spacing may have changed. Representation is separate from model quality, because a character spelled out as three rare byte tokens arrives as pieces the model has seen little of during training, and it makes the text longer. That length cost is the subject of the [fertility](#fertility) section.

## Vocabulary size
A bigger vocabulary can fit more common strings into single tokens. The effect on sequence length depends on the entries the tokenizer learns and the text it reads. The price is paid in two tables that grow with every entry. The embedding table $E$ has $V$ rows of $d$ numbers. The **output head** $W$ is the matrix at the end of the model that turns the final hidden vector $\mathbf{h}$ into one score per vocabulary entry, so it also has $V$ rows of $d$ numbers. Each score is a **logit** $z_j$, a raw number that a softmax later turns into a probability (see [Language Modeling](/tutorials/llm-fundamentals/language-modeling-and-next-token-prediction/)).

A **parameter** is one learned number in the model. The [story model](/tutorials/llm-fundamentals/#story-model) uses one matrix for both tables, a choice the [weight tying](#weight-tying) section explains, so its vocabulary costs a single table of $4{,}096 \times 384 = 1{,}572{,}864$ parameters. That is 14.3% of the model's 11,015,040. Growing to 16,384 entries makes the table $16{,}384 \times 384 = 6{,}291{,}456$, so the model grows by 4,718,592 parameters to 15,733,632 with an unchanged body, and the table becomes 40% of it.

The compute cost changes in two opposite directions. A **floating point operation**, or FLOP, is one multiplication or addition, and a forward pass costs about two FLOPs per parameter per token. Reading a row of $E$ costs no arithmetic, but the output head costs $2 \times d \times V$ [FLOPs per token](/tutorials/llm-fundamentals/the-transformer-block-and-model-anatomy/#flops-per-token), which is 3,145,728 at 4,096 and 12,582,912 at 16,384. The body of the story model is everything except the table. It has 9,442,176 parameters and costs about 18.88 million FLOPs per token, so each token costs about 22.03 million FLOPs at 4,096 and 31.47 million at 16,384, about 43% more. At this width the head would cost as much as the whole body at about 24,600 entries.

Each doubling of the vocabulary buys a smaller cut in tokens per word, while the head's cost per token doubles every time. Going from 512 to 1,024 entries cuts tokens per word from 2.96 to 2.53, about 15%. Going from 8,192 to 16,384 cuts it only from 2.180 to 2.173, while the head's cost doubles from 6.3 to 12.6 million FLOPs per token. On these stories total compute is lowest at 2,048 entries. The story model's 4,096 costs 3% more per word than that and buys 4% shorter sequences, so more of each story fits in the 512-token context. The sweep stops at 16,384 because 20,000 stories hold only 13,236 distinct pieces, and after 17,013 merges each of them is a single token with nothing left to merge.

Where the minimum falls depends on the text and on the width. TinyStories uses a small vocabulary of simple words, so merges stop paying early. Web text has many more strings frequent enough to deserve a merge, so it keeps rewarding a bigger vocabulary for longer. Width matters because the head grows with $d \times V$ while the body grows with $d^2$. On Llama 2 7B, at width 4,096, the 32,000-entry head costs 262 million FLOPs per token next to a body of about 13 billion, so quadrupling the vocabulary adds only 6% per token, and Meta's 15% fewer tokens more than pay for it. Shorter sequences also save attention work and context space that the figure leaves out, which is one reason recent multilingual models choose 128,000 to 256,000 entries.

## Fertility
A tokenizer learns its pieces from its training corpus, so it learns long pieces for the languages that dominate that corpus and has to build other languages from short pieces, sometimes a few letters or bytes at a time. **Fertility** $F$ is the average number of tokens per word for one language under one tokenizer, and it measures that gap. A language the tokenizer covers well has a fertility close to 1, while a poorly covered one can need several times as many tokens per word.

One sentence, "The doctor said the test results will be ready next week", and its translations show how far fertility can spread. GPT-2's vocabulary was trained on mostly English text. It spends 12 tokens on the English sentence, a fertility of about 1.1 for its 11 words, but 79 tokens on Russian and 94 on Hindi, 6.6 and 7.8 times as many. The gap comes from the tokenizer's training corpus rather than from the languages. Qwen2.5's vocabulary of 151,665 pieces was trained on multilingual text, and it spends 20 tokens on Russian, a quarter of GPT-2's count. It spends only 8 on Chinese, fewer than on English, because it learned whole Chinese words as single pieces. Spanish and German share the English alphabet, yet GPT-2 still spends 2.4 times as many tokens on each, because its merges are English ones. Hindi stays the most expensive language for both tokenizers, at 58 tokens or 4.8 times English for Qwen2.5. Each of its characters takes 3 bytes in UTF-8, so a character that a byte-level tokenizer has not merged into a larger piece costs up to 3 tokens.

![Fertility across languages](/images/llm-fundamentals/15b-fertility-across-languages.png)

The ratio of a language's token count to English for the same meaning is sometimes called its **token premium**, and [Petrov et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.15425) measured premiums above 10 for some language and tokenizer pairs. The [story model](/tutorials/llm-fundamentals/#story-model)'s tokenizer shows the extreme case. It learned its merges from English children's stories only, so it has no merge for any Hindi byte. It spends 24 tokens on the English sentence and 159 on the Hindi one, one token per byte, which is 6.6 times as many. Its 512-token context therefore holds only as much Hindi as English would fill in about 77 tokens, and each of those tokens costs the same 22.0 million FLOPs. The same multiple applies to every per-token cost. Under GPT-2's tokenizer an answer that takes 130 tokens in English takes about 1,014 in Hindi, and an application programming interface, or API, billed per token charges the Hindi user about 7.8 times as much for the same content. One sentence is a small sample, so a fertility number worth quoting comes from real traffic in each language.

## Special tokens and chat templates

A model reading a conversation needs to know where one message ends and the next begins, and who wrote each one. Plain text has no reliable markers for that, so tokenizers reserve a few ids that never come from ordinary text. These are **special tokens**, each a single id with a fixed job.

The common ones are the **beginning of sequence token** and the **end of sequence token**, abbreviated BOS and EOS. Llama 2 writes them as `<s>` and `</s>`, and the [story model](/tutorials/llm-fundamentals/#story-model) reserves IDs 256 and 257 for them, right after the 256 byte values. BOS marks the start of a document. The model learns to emit EOS when it is finished, so generation stops there. A **padding token** fills unused positions when sequences of different lengths are stacked into one batch, and it is excluded from the loss. The story model needs no separate one. It fills the unused end of a packed row with EOS and marks those positions so the loss skips them. Chat models add **role markers**, special tokens that announce a system, user or assistant message.

A **chat template** is the exact rule that turns a list of messages into one token sequence, including the role markers, the newlines between them and the place where the assistant's reply starts. The story model's template is the simplest kind. It has two role markers, USER at ID 258 and ASSISTANT at ID 259, and no system message. A request becomes BOS, USER, the request's tokens and ASSISTANT, and the model then writes the story and ends it with EOS. The request "Use these words: kite, duck." costs 13 tokens, so the prompt is 16 tokens, 3 of them special. "Use" splits into "U" and "se", because the capitalized word is rare in the stories. The tokenizer only ever outputs byte IDs and merge IDs, so no text can produce IDs 256 to 259, and the model can rely on them as boundaries. When the notebook generates text, it also blocks BOS, USER and ASSISTANT, so the model can end its reply with EOS but never open a new turn. Larger chat models follow the same pattern with more roles. Under Qwen2.5's ChatML template, a three message conversation becomes 26 tokens, and 6 of them are single special tokens, three `<|im_start|>` and three `<|im_end|>`.

The same request written in Llama 2's `[INST]` format and sent to the story model becomes 31 tokens with no special tokens at all. `<s>`, `[INST]` and `[/INST]` are not entries in its vocabulary, so the tokenizer spells them out of single symbols and capital letters. The length nearly doubles, since the stories never taught the tokenizer a merge for "INST". The bigger change is that the boundaries are now made of the same pieces any story or web page could contain.

![Chat template tokens](/images/llm-fundamentals/15b-chat-template-tokens.png)

The model learned the meaning of its role markers during **fine-tuning**, the extra training on conversations that turns a raw text predictor into an assistant (see [Post-Training](/tutorials/llm-fundamentals/post-training-from-base-model-to-assistant/)). The story model's pretraining documents hold only BOS, story tokens and EOS, so its base checkpoint never sees USER or ASSISTANT, and the markers carry meaning only after fine-tuning. With the wrong template the markers it relies on are absent, so it sees a conversation shaped like no example it was trained on. Nothing raises an error, since every string still tokenizes. Instead quality drops quietly. The model may ignore the system message, continue writing the user's turn, or never emit its end token and keep generating. Smaller mismatches do the same damage, such as a missing newline after the assistant marker, a BOS token added twice, or a fine-tuning set formatted with one template and served with another.

## Embedding lookup

Once text is a list of token ids, the network needs numbers it can compute with. An id such as 1,532 carries no meaning as a quantity, since token 1,532 is not "more" than token 1,531. The id only says which entry of the vocabulary the token is. **Embedding lookup** uses it as the index of a row in the embedding table $E$, and returns that row $\mathbf{e}_i$ as the token's vector of $d$ numbers.

Feeding the id in as a plain number would mislead the model, because ids are only labels handed out in a fixed order. GPT-2's tokenizer numbers its 256 bytes first and then each merged piece in the order it was learned. The [byte pair encoding](#byte-pair-encoding) section's toy tokenizer is numbered the same way. It gives its 8 letters ids 0 to 7 and its seven merges ids 8 to 14, so "we" is 8, "lo" is 9 and "low" is 14. As plain numbers, "we" and "lo" would look almost the same although they share no letter. Meanwhile "lo" and "low" share two letters but would sit five apart. A row lookup avoids this. Every token gets its own row, unrelated to the rows of its neighbours in the list. Training is free to place any two rows close together or far apart.

The lookup can be written as a matrix product, which is how it fits into the same algebra as the rest of the network. A **one-hot vector** $\mathbf{o}_i$ has $V$ entries, 1 in position $i$ and 0 elsewhere. Multiplying it by $E$ gives $\mathbf{o}_i E = \mathbf{e}_i$, since each output coordinate adds up one-hot entries times table entries and only the entry for token $i$ is nonzero.

The product form matters for training, since it makes the table an ordinary weight matrix that is adjusted like every other layer of the network. In the [story model](/tutorials/llm-fundamentals/#story-model) the table has 4,096 rows of 384 numbers. The rows start as small random numbers, and training moves each one until it holds whatever about its token helps predict the next token, which is the geometry the [embedding geometry](#embedding-geometry) section examines. A 512-token context becomes 512 rows stacked into a $512 \times 384$ matrix, and that matrix is what the first layer reads. It holds 196,608 numbers, or about 0.79 MB at 4 bytes each in float32, the format the story model trains in. The lookup itself does no arithmetic. Its cost is memory. The table holds the 1,572,864 parameters that the [vocabulary size](#vocabulary-size) section counted, which take about 6.3 MB in float32. Each lookup reads one row of 384 numbers, or 1,536 bytes, and one context reads at most 512 of the 4,096 rows.

The product form also explains how training treats the table. Training adjusts every learned number by following its **gradient**, the direction in which a small change to that number would reduce the prediction error. A row whose token did not appear in a batch was multiplied by zero in the lookup, so the lookup gives it a zero gradient and no learning signal, which is why rare tokens learn slowly. In one 512-token row of the story model, at most 512 of the 4,096 rows can receive a signal through the lookup, so at least 3,584 get none from that side. The story model also uses this table as its output head (the [weight tying](#weight-tying) section), so every row still gets a small gradient from the head, which lowers the scores of tokens that did not come next. The embedding of a token is the same wherever the token appears in the text, so the model learns position separately (see [Attention](/tutorials/llm-fundamentals/attention-and-positional-information/)).

## Embedding geometry

After training, the rows of the embedding table are not random. Tokens used in similar ways tend to end up with similar rows, as "cat" and "dog" do. Measuring "similar rows" needs a number that compares two vectors.

The **dot product** of two vectors $\mathbf{u}$ and $\mathbf{v}$ multiplies matching coordinates and adds the results. It grows when the vectors point the same way and when they are long. **Cosine similarity** divides the dot product by both lengths, which leaves only the angle $\theta$ between them.

$$
\cos\theta = \frac{\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v}}{\lVert \mathbf{u} \rVert \, \lVert \mathbf{v} \rVert}
$$

Here $\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v}$ is the dot product and $\lVert \mathbf{u} \rVert$ is the length of $\mathbf{u}$, the square root of the sum of its squared coordinates. Cosine similarity runs from 1 for the same direction, through 0 for perpendicular vectors, to −1 for opposite directions.

Three toy embeddings in two dimensions keep the arithmetic small. They are cat (3, 1), dog (2, 2) and car (−1, 3). The dot product of cat and dog is $3 \times 2 + 1 \times 2 = 8$, the lengths are $\sqrt{10} \approx 3.162$ and $\sqrt{8} \approx 2.828$, so the cosine is $8 / 8.944 \approx 0.894$, an angle of about 27 degrees. The dot product of cat and car is $3 \times (-1) + 1 \times 3 = 0$, so the cosine is 0 and the vectors are perpendicular. Doubling cat to (6, 2) doubles its dot product with dog to 16 but leaves the cosine at 0.894, which is why similarity search between embeddings usually uses cosine.

![Cosine similarity](/images/llm-fundamentals/15b-cosine-similarity.png)

Training produces this geometry through the output scores. The logit for token $j$ is the dot product $z_j = \mathbf{h} \cdot \mathbf{w}_j$, where $\mathbf{w}_j$ is row $j$ of the output head $W$. Tokens that are good continuations in the same contexts are pushed toward the same hidden vectors, so their rows drift toward each other. Input embedding rows drift together for a similar reason, since tokens used in similar contexts receive similar gradients. So cat and dog end up closer than cat and car. Trained tables are often anisotropic. **Anisotropy** means most rows share a common direction, so two unrelated tokens often have a positive cosine such as 0.3. A raw cosine has to be compared against that baseline before it means anything. Input embedding rows are a starting representation, and the vectors used for retrieval come from later layers (see [LLM Applications](/tutorials/llm-fundamentals/using-llms-in-applications/)).

## Weight tying

The embedding table and the output head do mirror image jobs. One turns a token id into a vector, the other scores how well a vector matches each token, and both are $V$ by $d$ matrices with one row per token. It is natural to ask whether the model needs two copies of what is nearly the same table.

**Weight tying** uses one matrix for both, so the output head is the embedding table and $W = E$. The logit for token $j$ becomes $z_j = \mathbf{h} \cdot \mathbf{e}_j$, the dot product of the final hidden vector with token $j$'s own embedding. [Press and Wolf](https://arxiv.org/abs/1608.05859) showed that tying often improves language models at no extra cost, since every training step now updates the shared rows from both ends. The [story model](/tutorials/llm-fundamentals/#story-model) ties its tables, which saves 1,572,864 parameters. An untied model of the same shape would have 12,587,904, so tying removes 12.5% of them, along with 6.3 MB of float32 weights. It saves no compute, since the head still multiplies $\mathbf{h}$ by every row.

The saving matters more for small models and large vocabularies. A model of width 2,048 with 128,000 entries has 262,144,000 parameters in each table, about a quarter of a 1B model. So small models such as GPT-2 and Llama 3.2 1B tie their tables, and the Gemma family ties them at every size. Llama 2 7B keeps its two $32{,}000 \times 4{,}096$ tables separate, since tying would save only 1.9% of its 6.74 billion parameters, and lets the input and output roles specialize. The tradeoff and other forms of parameter sharing are developed in [Parameter Efficient Adaptation](/tutorials/llm-deep-dive/parameter-efficient-adaptation/#sharing-levels).
