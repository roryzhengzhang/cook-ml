An H100 can do 989 trillion floating point operations a second. Generating text for one user with the 7B reference model uses about 3.4 trillion of them, roughly a third of one percent. The rest of the chip sits idle while the weights stream in from memory. A serving system is the machinery that fills that idle arithmetic with other users' work while every user still gets a fast first token and a steady stream after it.

That job turns into a sizing question. A product sends the 70B model 50 requests a second and promises a first token within a second and a new token at least every 30 ms. Each request has a 1,000-token prompt and a 500-token reply. Run one request at a time on an eight-GPU replica, each request holds its replica for about 3.4 seconds of decoding, so the traffic needs about 170 replicas, 1,360 GPUs. The plan worked out at the end of the chapter needs 40, and the gap comes from how the system batches, schedules, caches and stores numbers. The figure shows one request's path through such a system and what runs out at each layer.

![Serving stack](/images/llm-deep-dive/16h-serving-stack.png)

Each layer of the stack runs out of something different, and the chapter takes them in the order they bind. Inside one GPU the limits are arithmetic for prefill and bandwidth for decode. The roofline model places both phases against those two limits and shows why decode leaves most of the arithmetic idle. Cache reads then show why batching alone cannot fix that at long context, and splitting the 70B across eight GPUs sets the per-GPU numbers every later section uses. With the limits known, the scheduler layer handles their interference, where one long prompt's arithmetic delays every decoding stream. Chunked prefill softens it, and running prefill and decode on separate machines removes it. The memory layer comes next, because cache capacity sets how large a batch can grow. Prefix caching and cache tiers avoid recomputing and storing the same tokens twice, the paged allocator shares and reclaims blocks, and number formats shrink weights, activations and cache. Speculative decoding spends idle arithmetic on extra tokens, and the engines show how all of this is packaged on one replica. The last sections step out to the fleet. Goodput and a capacity plan turn the per-replica numbers into the 40 GPUs that answer the opening question, and a diagnosis table maps symptoms back to the sections that fix them.

## The roofline model

A GPU has two separate speed limits, one for arithmetic and one for moving bytes from memory. A computation runs into whichever it reaches first. The **roofline model** draws both limits on one chart. Williams, Waterman and Patterson introduced it in 2009. The horizontal axis is [arithmetic intensity](/tutorials/llm-fundamentals/inference-and-decoding/#memory-bandwidth-bound-decoding), the FLOPs performed per byte read. The vertical axis is the attainable FLOPs per second. A computation with intensity $I$ can run no faster than $\min(F, \beta I)$, where $F$ is peak compute and $\beta$ is memory bandwidth. The chart therefore has a sloped roof on the left and a flat roof on the right. The two meet at the ridge point $F/\beta$.

The model's value is that it places a workload before anyone writes a kernel. Decode with empty contexts reads the weights once per step and does $2N$ FLOPs per sequence, where $N$ is the parameter count. With $b$ sequences in the batch and 2 bytes per weight, the intensity is $2Nb / 2N = b$. Prefill of $n$ tokens has intensity close to $n$. The H100 numbers in this chapter are 989 TFLOP/s of dense bf16 arithmetic, 3.35 TB/s of bandwidth and 80 GB of memory, all assumptions about one H100 SXM.

The walkthrough idealizes both roofs. Real kernels reach perhaps 70% to 90% of peak bandwidth and less of peak arithmetic, so the practical ridge moves and its position has to be measured. The shape survives, though. Decode at the batch sizes servers actually run sits on the sloped roof, and prefill sits on the flat one. The Fundamentals chapter derives the 4.0 ms floor itself. What the roofline adds is the reason almost every technique in this chapter is about either raising decode's intensity or keeping prefill from stealing decode's time.

## Cache reads
The walkthrough above ignored the cache, and real contexts make that wrong quickly. Every sequence reads its own keys and values at every step, and unlike the weights, that read is not shared across the batch. For the 7B with 16 sequences at 4,096 tokens each, the cache is $16 \times 2 \text{ GiB} = 34.4$ GB against 13.48 GB of weights. The step reads 47.8 GB in 14.3 ms, and 72% of those bytes are cache. Adding more sequences multiplies the cache term while the weight term stays fixed.

With the cache included, decode's intensity becomes

$$
I(b) = \frac{2Nb}{2N + b\,t\,c},
$$

where $b$ is the batch size, $t$ the tokens of context per sequence, $c$ the cache bytes per token, and $2N$ both the FLOPs per token and the bytes of bf16 weights. As $b$ grows, the intensity stops rising and approaches $2N/(tc)$. For the 7B at 4,096 tokens that ceiling is

$$
\frac{13.48 \times 10^9 \text{ FLOPs}}{4{,}096 \times 524{,}288 \text{ bytes}} = 6.3 \text{ FLOPs per byte}.
$$

No batch size can push this model's decode anywhere near the ridge at 295. The 70B with [grouped-query attention](/tutorials/llm-fundamentals/attention-and-positional-information/#head-sharing) stores 320 KiB per token, so the same division gives a ceiling of 103 at 4,096 tokens, still below the ridge but sixteen times higher.

![Cache share of decode bytes](/images/llm-deep-dive/16h-cache-share-of-decode-bytes.png)

Attention itself explains the ceiling. For each cached token, a query head spends about 4 FLOPs per head dimension, 2 to score the key and 2 to weight the value. All heads together therefore spend $4d$ FLOPs per layer, where $d$ is the model width. Reading that token's key and value costs $4 h_{\text{kv}} d_h$ bytes in bf16, where $h_{\text{kv}}$ is the number of key-value heads and $d_h$ their width. The ratio is $h / h_{\text{kv}}$, the number of query heads sharing each key-value head. That ratio is 1 for the 7B and 8 for the 70B, and batching cannot change it, because every sequence's attention reads its own cache. Decode attention is therefore memory bound by construction, and the only levers are fewer bytes per cached token or fewer cached tokens read per step.

Both levers now shape model architecture, which [Efficient Attention](/tutorials/llm-deep-dive/efficient-attention-mechanisms/) covers at the kernel and design level. Grouped-query attention and multi-head latent attention cut the bytes per token. DeepSeek's [V3.2 report](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.02556) cuts the tokens read. Its **DeepSeek Sparse Attention** adds a small "lightning indexer" that scores every earlier token with a few cheap heads, which can run in FP8. The main attention then reads only the top 2,048 key-value entries per query. Core attention cost falls from growing with $L^2$ to growing with $Lk$, where $L$ is the context length and $k = 2{,}048$. At a 128K context the main attention reads 2,048 of 131,072 cached tokens, about 1.6%. The indexer still touches every token, so its cost still grows with $L^2$, but at a far smaller constant. The design is an example of serving cost at long context driving the architecture.

## Tensor and pipeline parallelism
The 70B's weights take 137.95 GB, so they do not fit on one 80 GB GPU, and the question is how to cut them. **Tensor parallelism** splits every weight matrix across the GPUs, so each GPU holds a slice of every layer and the partial results are summed by an all-reduce twice per layer. This scheme follows [Megatron-LM](https://arxiv.org/abs/1909.08053). **Pipeline parallelism** gives each GPU a contiguous run of whole layers and passes activations from one stage to the next. [Parallelism](/tutorials/llm-deep-dive/distributed-training-and-parallelism/) develops both for training. Serving changes the tradeoff, because decode is memory bound and latency counts.

Memory decides the minimum width. With 10% of each GPU held back as margin, 72 GB is usable, and the table shows what tensor parallelism of width 2, 4 and 8 leaves for cache. Under width 8 each GPU holds one of the eight key-value heads, so its cache costs 40,960 bytes per token.

| Tensor-parallel width | Weights per GPU | Cache room per GPU | Cache tokens across the group | 4,096-token sequences |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | 68.98 GB | 3.02 GB | 18,453 | 4 |
| 4 | 34.49 GB | 37.51 GB | 457,906 | 111 |
| 8 | 17.24 GB | 54.76 GB | 1,336,812 | 326 |

Doubling the GPUs from four to eight triples the cache capacity, because the weights' share of each GPU halves while the margin stays fixed. Width 8 is also the natural limit for this model. With eight key-value heads, a width of 16 would have to store each head on two GPUs, so cache capacity per GPU would stop improving.

Latency then separates the two layouts. Under tensor parallelism all eight GPUs read their 17.24 GB at once, which takes 5.15 ms. The 160 all-reduces per token add about 1.6 ms if each costs 10 microseconds at batch 1, a teaching estimate for small messages on NVLink. A token takes about 6.8 ms. Under eight pipeline stages the same token passes through the stages one after another, and each stage reads 17.24 GB, so the token takes $8 \times 5.15 = 41.2$ ms.

![Tensor and pipeline timelines](/images/llm-deep-dive/16h-tensor-and-pipeline-timelines.png)

The usual rule says "tensor parallelism for latency, pipeline parallelism for throughput". The numbers show where it holds. In memory-bound decode, each pipeline micro-batch re-reads every stage's weights, so one weight read serves only one micro-batch rather than the whole batch. At equal concurrency the pipeline therefore produces tokens about six times more slowly, and it only catches up with a batch eight times larger, which the cache rarely has room for. Pipelining wins where the work is compute bound and communication is the cost. A 2,048-token prefill under width-8 tensor parallelism sends 160 all-reduces of 33.6 MB each. Unless the transfers overlap with compute, they take about 21 ms at NVLink speed against 89 ms of arithmetic. A pipeline sends one 33.6 MB activation per stage boundary. Across nodes InfiniBand gives about 50 GB/s per GPU instead of 450, so tensor parallelism's per-layer all-reduces become much slower. Large deployments therefore usually run tensor parallelism inside a node and pipeline stages or separate replicas across nodes.

A [mixture of experts](/tutorials/llm-fundamentals/the-transformer-block-and-model-anatomy/#mixture-of-experts) changes the roofline, because the bytes read per step depend on which experts the batch touches. The reference MoE stores 93.41 GB but activates 12.9B parameters per token, which take 25.8 GB. At batch 1 a step reads about 25.8 GB. With top-2 routing over 8 experts and roughly even routing, an expert goes unused by $b$ tokens with probability $0.75^b$. At batch 8 that is 0.10, so 90% of expert weights are read and a step reads 84.4 GB. At batch 16 it reads 92.5 GB, nearly everything. Decode at modest batch therefore pays for total parameters in bandwidth and active parameters in FLOPs. Each expert sees only about $2b/8 = b/4$ tokens, so experts reach the ridge near $b = 4 \times 295 \approx 1{,}180$ tokens per step, four times the dense model's batch.

That arithmetic explains why large MoE models are served with **expert parallelism**. Experts are spread across many GPUs and tokens are sent to them with an all-to-all exchange, so the global batch can be large enough to feed every expert. DeepSeek's V3 and R1 activate 8 of 256 routed experts per layer. Their [published inference system](https://github.com/deepseek-ai/open-infra-index/blob/main/202502OpenSourceWeek/day_6_one_more_thing_deepseekV3R1_inference_system_overview.md) runs prefill units across 4 nodes with expert parallelism of 32 and decode units across 18 nodes with expert parallelism of 144. Each decode GPU holds 2 routed experts and 1 shared expert. With each expert seeing about 8/256 of the tokens, a decode step needs thousands of tokens in flight across the group to keep expert matrix multiplications efficient. The same report gives about 73.7k input tokens per second per H800 node during prefill (counting cache hits) and about 14.8k output tokens per second per node during decode. ## Chunked prefill

[Continuous batching](/tutorials/llm-fundamentals/inference-and-decoding/#continuous-batching-and-paged-attention) lets a new request join the running batch at any step, and that creates the interference this section is about. A new request needs its prefill before it can decode. If the scheduler runs the whole prompt in one iteration, every sequence already decoding waits for it. **Chunked prefill** splits a long prompt into pieces and runs one piece per iteration alongside the decode tokens. [SARATHI](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.16369) introduced it, and [Sarathi-Serve](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.02310) developed it into a stall-free scheduler.

The stall comes from the two phases sitting on opposite sides of the roofline. An iteration lasts as long as its slowest part, and every sequence in the batch receives its next token only when the iteration ends, so the iteration time is each stream's gap between tokens. A decode iteration is short because one weight read bounds it and most of the arithmetic sits idle. A 2,048-token prompt is far past the ridge. Its matrix multiplications alone take about 70 ms on the 7B at 40% of peak, so an iteration that carries the whole prompt lasts at least that long, however few decode tokens ride with it. Chunking caps how much prefill work any one iteration may hold, and a small chunk also fills arithmetic that decode leaves idle, so part of the prompt costs almost nothing extra.

The split is exact because attention is causal. Each chunk writes its keys and values into the cache, and the next chunk attends to them along with its own tokens, the same way a decode token attends to everything before it. When the last chunk finishes, the cache and the first output token are the ones an unchunked prefill would have produced. The price falls on the new request. Its prompt waits for several iteration slots, and each later chunk reads the cache that earlier chunks wrote, so the first token arrives somewhat later than it would from one large iteration. The chunk size therefore trades the new request's TTFT against every other stream's TPOT, and the schedule below prices that trade on one GPU.

![Chunked prefill iterations](/images/llm-deep-dive/16h-chunked-prefill-iterations.png)

Each 512-token chunk of the prompt rides in one iteration next to the 32 decode tokens, so every stream still gets a token at the end of each iteration. Below each iteration, the prompt's cache gains one chunk, and the arrows show the new chunk attending to the chunks already cached, which is why the split gives the same result as one large prefill.

The chunk size is a dial between time to first token (TTFT) and time per output token (TPOT), and the roofline from the [roofline model](#the-roofline-model) section says where its useful range starts. A chunk of 128 tokens plus 32 decode tokens needs 5.8 ms of arithmetic, less than the 6.59 ms memory read. The prefill therefore rides on decode's weight read nearly for free. Decode streams then see no slowdown at all, and the prompt finishes after 16 iterations at 105 ms. Chunks of 512 finish it at 77 ms but stretch each decode gap to about 19 ms. Below roughly the ridge, a chunk wastes nothing. Above it, every extra prompt token costs decode streams time. Schedulers express the dial as a **token budget**, the maximum number of tokens one iteration may process, counting both prompt chunks and decode tokens. In [vLLM V1](https://vllm.ai/blog/2025-01-27-v1-alpha-release) the scheduler drops the separate prefill and decode phases altogether. Each step is a mapping from request to number of tokens under one budget, which covers chunked prefill, prefix-cache hits and speculative tokens with the same mechanism.

## Prefill and decode disaggregation

Chunking makes the two phases share a GPU more politely, but they still share it. **Prefill-decode disaggregation** runs them on separate pools of GPUs. Prefill workers compute the prompt's cache, send it over the network to a decode worker, and the decode worker streams the reply. [DistServe](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.09670) argued that colocation forces one parallel layout and one batch policy on two workloads that want different ones. It optimizes **goodput**, the request rate a system can serve while meeting both its TTFT and TPOT targets. Against the colocated systems it compared, it reports serving 7.4 times more requests or meeting 12.6 times tighter targets. [Splitwise](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.18677) made the same split to match each phase to suitable hardware.

The new cost is moving the cache. For the 70B, a 2,048-token prompt produces $2{,}048 \times 327{,}680 = 671$ MB of cache. If both pools use width-8 tensor parallelism, each GPU sends its own 83.9 MB shard over its own InfiniBand link at about 50 GB/s. That takes 1.7 ms, against 89 ms to compute the prefill at 40% of peak. The transfer is under 2% of the work, and it can stream layer by layer while later layers are still computing. The 7B with full multi-head attention shows the opposite case. The same prompt's 1.07 GB on one 50 GB/s link takes 21.5 ms against a 70 ms prefill, almost a third. Grouped-query attention is part of what makes disaggregation cheap.

Disaggregation buys three things. Decode streams never wait behind a prompt. Each pool can use its own layout, with prefill sized for TTFT and decode packed for bandwidth. And the pools scale separately in proportion to the traffic's prompt and output lengths. It costs a second scheduling decision, the transfer path, and some idle capacity when the mix shifts. Production systems now build on it:

- [Mooncake](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.00079) is the serving platform for Moonshot AI's Kimi. It separates prefill and decode clusters and builds a distributed cache store from the cluster's otherwise underused CPU memory, DRAM and SSD. Its scheduler is organized around where cache lives, and it rejects requests early under overload. The authors report that it lets Kimi handle 75% more requests on real workloads.
- [NVIDIA Dynamo](https://github.com/ai-dynamo/dynamo) is an orchestration layer above vLLM, SGLang and TensorRT-LLM. It runs prefill and decode as separately scaled pools, routes requests by worker load and cache overlap, autoscales the pools against latency targets, and offloads cache from GPU to CPU, SSD and remote storage.
- DeepSeek's inference system for V3 and R1 runs prefill and decode on separate units with different degrees of expert parallelism. The deep dive of the [tensor and pipeline parallelism](#tensor-and-pipeline-parallelism) section describes it.

## Prefix caching
Many requests begin with identical tokens, such as a shared system prompt, few-shot examples or the earlier turns of a conversation. Attention is causal, so the keys and values of a prefix depend only on that prefix. **Prefix caching** keeps them after a request finishes, and any later request that starts with the same tokens skips recomputing them. vLLM hashes each full cache block together with all tokens before it, so equal prefixes map to equal blocks. [SGLang's RadixAttention](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.07104) keeps a **radix tree** over every cached sequence, so requests share whatever depth they agree on. A radix tree is a tree whose edges are runs of tokens. When memory runs short, RadixAttention evicts the least recently used leaves.

The same causal property decides how a lookup works. The key and value stored for a token depend on that token and on every token before it, because each layer's attention mixes in the earlier positions. Sixteen identical tokens therefore have different cache entries when they follow different prefixes, and a block can be reused only if everything before it matches too. Hashing a block together with its whole history encodes exactly that condition. A new request walks its blocks from the start and stops at the first miss. [vLLM caches only full blocks](https://docs.vllm.ai/en/latest/design/prefix_caching.html), so a prefix shared for 1,030 tokens reuses 64 blocks of 16, or 1,024 tokens. The last 6 shared tokens are computed again with the rest of the prompt.

A hit saves prefill arithmetic in proportion to the matched length, and the request's TTFT shrinks to the time needed for its unmatched suffix. The cost is memory. On the 7B each cached token holds 0.5 MiB, so a 1,024-token prefix occupies 512 MiB that running requests could otherwise use. Engines keep that cost out of the way of live work. In vLLM a finished request's blocks go back to the free queue with their contents intact, and they stay reusable until the allocator hands them to another request, oldest first. Cached prefixes therefore live in memory that would otherwise sit empty, and the hit rate falls when heavy load leaves little memory free.

The economics favor caching a shared prefix by a wide margin. At 20 requests per second, the 1,024-token system prompt is 20,480 tokens per second of prefill, 70% of what one H100 prefills for the 7B at 40% of peak. Keeping it costs 512 MiB once. A cache hit requires an exact token match from the first token, so anything that varies early in the prompt wipes out every block after it. A timestamp or a user name placed before the instructions is enough. Prompt templates put static text first for that reason. API providers price the difference directly. DeepSeek's report lists R1 input tokens at $0.14 per million on a cache hit against $0.55 on a miss, and says 56.3% of the input tokens it served in one day hit its on-disk cache.

Routing decides whether those hits happen. A conversation's next turn helps only if it reaches the replica that holds its prefix, and with four replicas and random routing that happens 25% of the time. **Prefix-aware routing** sends requests to the replica with the longest cached match, and Dynamo's router weighs that match against each worker's load. Load still matters, because routing purely by cache would pile popular prefixes onto one replica. Prompt length matters too. Four requests with 4,000, 200, 4,000 and 200 prompt tokens sent round-robin to two 7B replicas give one replica both long prompts, so the second waits behind the first and sees a TTFT of 273 ms. Routing by outstanding tokens pairs each long prompt with a short one, and the worst TTFT drops to 143 ms.

**Cache tiering** extends prefix caching past GPU memory. Evicted blocks move to CPU memory or SSD instead of being discarded, and come back on a hit. [LMCache](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.09665) is such a cache layer. It plugs into engines such as vLLM, offloads cache to CPU and storage, reuses prefixes across queries and engines, and moves cache between prefill and decode workers. Mooncake's store and Dynamo's block manager play the same role. Reloading beats recomputing whenever the transfer is shorter than the prefill. Assume a PCIe host link of about 50 GB/s for an H100. Over it, the 7B's 1,024-token prefix reloads in 10.7 ms against 34.9 ms to recompute it. For the 70B at width 8, each GPU reloads only its 42 MB shard in 0.8 ms against 44.6 ms of prefill. The fewer bytes a model caches per token of compute, the more reloading pays.

## Paged memory
[PagedAttention](/tutorials/llm-fundamentals/inference-and-decoding/#continuous-batching-and-paged-attention) stores the cache in fixed blocks and keeps a block table per request. Its benefits show up as three memory numbers. A request that reserves 4,096 slots up front but stops at 1,100 tokens uses 27% of its reservation. Paging wastes only the unfilled tail of the last block. That is $(B-1)/2 = 7.5$ slots on average for blocks of $B = 16$ tokens, or 0.7% of a 1,100-token request. The block size trades that waste against kernel efficiency. Larger blocks make attention's memory reads longer and more regular, but waste more slots and share prefixes in coarser units.

Sharing is the second benefit. Each block carries a **reference count**, the number of block tables pointing at it. **Copy on write** lets several sequences share blocks until one of them needs to change a shared block, and only then gives that sequence its own copy. **Parallel sampling** generates several answers to one prompt, and it is the clearest case. Four samples of a 1,000-token prompt on the 7B share 62 full blocks and one block holding 8 tokens. The first three samples to write a token into that partial block each copy it first, and the fourth writes in place. The prompt's cache costs $(63 + 3) \times 8 = 528$ MiB instead of $4 \times 63 \times 8 = 2{,}016$ MiB before the samples' own tokens accumulate. That is 74% less. Beam search uses the same mechanism. Beams share their common history, a pruned beam decrements its blocks' counts, and blocks return to the free list when the count reaches zero.

![Copy on write blocks](/images/llm-deep-dive/16h-copy-on-write-blocks.png)

**Preemption** is the third case. When the pool runs out mid-generation, the scheduler evicts a running sequence and resumes it later. It can either swap the sequence's blocks to CPU memory and back, or drop them and recompute the cache from the prompt and the tokens generated so far, in one prefill-like pass. For a 2,048-token sequence on the 7B, swapping moves 1.07 GB each way, which takes about 21 ms per direction at 50 GB/s. Recomputing takes about 70 ms of compute. For the 70B at width 8, swapping moves 84 MB per GPU in 1.7 ms each way, against about 89 ms of recompute. Swapping looks cheaper, but it needs CPU memory, competes with other host traffic, and moves many small blocks rather than one large buffer. Recomputing needs nothing extra and also reuses any prefix still cached. vLLM V1 [recomputes by default](https://docs.vllm.ai/en/stable/configuration/optimization/), citing its lower overhead in that architecture. Either way, frequent preemption means the system is admitting more work than its cache can hold, and admission control or a smaller per-request token limit is the real fix.

## Quantization formats
[Weight quantization](/tutorials/llm-fundamentals/inference-and-decoding/#quantization) shrinks the bytes decode reads, but which numbers get quantized decides where the speedup appears. **Weight-only quantization** stores weights in 4 bits and converts them back to bf16 inside the kernel, so the arithmetic still runs at bf16 speed. It is written W4A16, for 4-bit weights and 16-bit activations. [GPTQ](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.17323) and [AWQ](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.00978) are the common methods. **Weight-and-activation quantization**, written W8A8, stores both in 8 bits and runs the matrix multiplication on 8-bit tensor cores. On an H100 these do 1,979 dense FP8 TFLOP/s, twice the bf16 rate. The table shows what each buys for the 7B on the roofline.

| Format | Bytes read per step | Step at batch 1 | Step at batch 256 | Batch where decode turns compute bound |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| bf16 weights and activations | 13.48 GB | 4.02 ms | 4.02 ms | 295 |
| int4 weights, bf16 math, one 16-bit scale per 128 weights | 3.47 GB | 1.04 ms | 3.49 ms | about 76 |
| FP8 weights and activations | 6.74 GB | 2.01 ms | 2.01 ms | 295 |

Int4 weights give almost four times the speed at batch 1 but only 15% at batch 256, because the bf16 arithmetic becomes the limit at about a quarter of the batch. FP8 halves both the bytes and the arithmetic time, so it gives twice the speed at every batch size and also speeds up prefill. That is why weight-only int4 suits small-batch and on-device serving, while large deployments use FP8. DeepSeek serves V3 with FP8 matrix multiplications, matching how it was trained. Meta released an [FP8 version of Llama 3.1 405B](https://ai.meta.com/blog/meta-llama-3-1/) so the model fits on a single 8-GPU node.

Activations are harder to quantize than weights for a reason in the shape of a matrix multiplication. Each output is $y_{ij} = \sum_k x_{ik} w_{kj}$, where $i$ indexes tokens, $j$ output channels and $k$ the inner channels being summed. A scale that depends on the token $i$ or the output channel $j$ can be pulled outside the sum, so the inner products run on integers and are rescaled once at the end. A scale that depends on $k$ cannot. Large models develop a few inner channels whose activations are tens of times larger than the rest. These are the **outlier channels** documented by [LLM.int8()](https://arxiv.org/abs/2208.07339), and they sit exactly on the $k$ axis. A per-token scale has to stretch to cover the outlier, and the ordinary channels then fall below half a quantization step and round to zero.

[SmoothQuant](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.10438) moves the difficulty into the weights, which are static and can be quantized carefully offline. It divides activation channel $k$ by a factor $s_k$ and multiplies row $k$ of the weight matrix by the same $s_k$, which leaves every product unchanged. The factor $s_k = \sqrt{\max|X_k| / \max|W_k|}$ splits the range between the two. Here $\max|X_k|$ is the largest activation magnitude in channel $k$ and $\max|W_k|$ the largest weight magnitude in row $k$. A small case uses two tokens, $x_1 = (0.5, -1.0, 12.0, 0.25)$ and $x_2 = (-0.75, 0.5, -9.0, 1.0)$, with channel 3 as the outlier. The $4 \times 2$ weight matrix has rows $(0.4, -0.2)$, $(0.3, 0.5)$, $(0.1, 0.3)$ and $(-0.6, 0.2)$, and quantization uses 4-bit levels from $-7$ to $7$ with one scale per token. Token 1's scale is $12/7 = 1.71$, so 0.5 and 0.25 both round to zero and $-1.0$ becomes $-1.71$. The largest output error is 0.54, 16% of the output's size. The smoothing factors are 1.37, 1.41, 6.32 and 1.29, which shrink channel 3 to 1.90 and $-1.42$ while the other channels shrink much less. After smoothing, no value rounds to zero and the largest output error falls to 0.16, about 4%. Smoothing works by shrinking each token's scale. Token 1's half step falls from 0.86 to 0.14, so its small channels at 0.37 and 0.19 now clear it. The square root makes the split even. After smoothing, channel 3's largest activation and row 3's largest weight are both 1.90, so neither side keeps the whole range. The weights pay with that wider row, whose largest entry grows from 0.3 to 1.90, and they pay on the side that can be quantized offline.

**KV cache quantization** applies the same reasoning to the cache, which the [cache reads](#cache-reads) section showed dominates decode bytes at long context. Storing the cache in FP8 halves it, so the 70B on eight GPUs holds 652 sequences of 4,096 tokens instead of 326, and every long-context decode step reads half the cache bytes. vLLM's [quantized KV cache](https://docs.vllm.ai/en/latest/features/quantization/quantized_kvcache/) option stores it in FP8 E4M3 for roughly twice the tokens. Going lower runs into the same outlier problem in a new place. [KIVI](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.02750) found that keys should be quantized per channel and values per token, because key outliers sit in fixed channels while value errors spread across a token. With that split it reached 2-bit caches with almost unchanged quality on the models it tested. Quantized caches usually hurt long contexts, long reasoning chains and code first, so evaluation has to cover those tasks rather than one average score.

## Speculative decoding
[Speculative decoding](/tutorials/llm-fundamentals/inference-and-decoding/#speculative-decoding) verifies several drafted tokens in one target pass, and the variants differ in where the drafts come from. A **separate draft model** is a smaller model from the same family. It needs the same tokenizer and its own memory and scheduling. [Medusa](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.10774) adds several extra output heads to the target, each predicting a token further ahead from the last hidden state. It verifies a tree of their candidates with a tree-shaped attention mask. [EAGLE](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.15077) trains a single light layer that drafts autoregressively on the target's own hidden features. [EAGLE-3](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.01840) predicts tokens directly instead of features and fuses features from several of the target's layers. It reports speedups up to 6.5 times, and 1.38 times higher throughput at batch 64 inside SGLang. **Prompt lookup decoding** needs no model at all. It drafts by finding the last few generated tokens earlier in the prompt and copying what followed them there. That works well for summarization, retrieval answers and code edits, where the output repeats the input.

**Multi-token prediction**, or MTP, builds the drafter into training. DeepSeek-V3 trains one extra module that predicts the token after next alongside the usual next-token objective. The [V3 technical report](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.19437) reuses that module at inference as a one-token drafter and reports that the second token is accepted 85% to 90% of the time, giving 1.8 times the tokens per second. That figure is consistent with the expected-length formula from the Fundamentals chapter. With one draft per round, a round emits $1 + \alpha$ tokens on average, between 1.85 and 1.90 for $\alpha$ from 0.85 to 0.90. The verification pass costs slightly more than a plain step.

Every variant depends on the target pass being nearly free, and batching takes that away. Verifying $\gamma$ drafts processes $\gamma + 1$ tokens per sequence, so the arithmetic of a step grows by that factor while the weight read stays the same. For the 7B with $\alpha = 0.8$, $\gamma = 4$ and a draft model ten times smaller, the round yields 3.36 tokens. At batch 64 the 5-token verification needs 4.36 ms of arithmetic against the 4.02 ms read, and the speedup is 2.27 times. At batch 128 it falls to 1.31 times, and at batch 256 speculation is 29% slower than plain decoding, because the step is now compute bound and verified tokens get thrown away. The discarded share is $1 - 3.36/5$, about a third.

Those speedups come from pricing one round. A round costs the target's verification pass plus four steps of the draft. The draft model reads 1.35 GB per step and stays memory bound at every batch size here, so its four steps take 1.61 ms. At batch 1 the round takes $4.02 + 1.61 = 5.63$ ms for 3.36 tokens. That is 1.68 ms per token, which reproduces the Fundamentals chapter's 2.4 times. At batch 64 verification grows to 4.36 ms, and the round's 5.97 ms gives 1.78 ms per token against 4.02 ms for plain decoding. At batch 128 verification needs 8.72 ms of arithmetic, so a token costs 3.07 ms. At batch 256 verification alone takes 17.4 ms, and a token costs 5.67 ms. Below the ridge the four extra tokens per sequence ride on a weight read that happens anyway. Above it every verified token is paid for in arithmetic, including the ones that are rejected.

![Speculative speedup versus batch](/images/llm-deep-dive/16h-speculative-speedup-versus-batch.png)

Long contexts push the drop-off later. When cache reads dominate the step, verifying five tokens reads the same cache as verifying one, so the extra arithmetic stays hidden. In the figure's dashed curve the 7B at 1,024 tokens of context keeps a 3.3 times gain until its memory fills at batch 109. A serving system therefore treats the number of drafts as a function of load, shrinking $\gamma$ or turning speculation off as batches grow and the step turns compute bound.

## Serving engines
The techniques above reach production through a few open engines, each organized around a different idea. The engine runs the model on a set of GPUs. An orchestration layer such as Dynamo from the [prefill and decode disaggregation](#prefill-and-decode-disaggregation) section sits above several engines and routes, scales and moves cache between them. Serving many fine-tuned adapters on one shared base model is a separate scheduling problem, covered in [Efficient Adaptation](/tutorials/llm-deep-dive/parameter-efficient-adaptation/).

- **vLLM** introduced PagedAttention. Its V1 architecture uses one scheduler that assigns each request a number of tokens per step under a token budget, enables prefix caching by default after cutting its overhead to under 1% of throughput even at a 0% hit rate, and captures the model as piecewise CUDA graphs to relax the fixed-shape limit described below.
- **SGLang** introduced RadixAttention for automatic prefix sharing, and compressed finite state machines that let constrained decoding emit several forced tokens at once. Its paper reports up to 6.4 times higher throughput than the systems it compared against across the tasks it tested.
- **TensorRT-LLM** is NVIDIA's engine. It builds optimized engines for NVIDIA GPUs and provides [in-flight batching](https://nvidia.github.io/TensorRT-LLM/features/paged-attention-ifb-scheduler.html) over a paged cache. In-flight batching is its name for continuous batching.
- [FlashInfer](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.01005) is a kernel library rather than an engine. It stores the cache in a block-sparse format that covers paged blocks and shared prefixes, generates attention variants from templates at run time, and balances uneven sequence lengths across the GPU's compute units while staying compatible with CUDA graphs. SGLang, vLLM and MLC-Engine use it, and it reports 29% to 69% lower inter-token latency than compiler backends.

**CUDA graphs** matter more for decode than their name suggests. One decode step launches hundreds of small kernels, and at a 4 ms step the CPU-side cost of launching each kernel adds up. The CPU issues kernels one after another while the GPU runs them. A large matrix multiplication lasts long enough to hide the next launch, but normalizations, additions and rotary embeddings finish in a few microseconds, and when a kernel ends before the CPU has issued the next one, the GPU waits. The smaller the batch, the shorter every kernel, so the waste is worst where decode latency matters most. A CUDA graph records the sequence of launches once and replays it with a single call. Graphs need fixed shapes, so engines capture one graph per batch-size bucket.

A batch that falls between buckets is padded up to the smallest captured graph that covers it. According to vLLM's [CUDA graph design notes](https://docs.vllm.ai/en/latest/design/cuda_graphs.html), it runs the step without a graph when none fits. On the sloped roof the padding is nearly free. A 7B batch of 37 padded to 40 still reads the same 13.48 GB of weights, so the step stays at 4.02 ms, and the three empty rows use only arithmetic that was idle anyway. Attention is the awkward part, because its work depends on each sequence's context length, which changes every step. vLLM's piecewise mode therefore captures everything between the attention calls as graphs and runs attention itself eagerly, which keeps every attention backend usable.

## Capacity planning
A service is judged by requests that meet their targets, not by total tokens. A **service level objective**, or SLO, is a measurable target such as a 99th-percentile TTFT under 1 second and a TPOT under 30 ms. Goodput from the [prefill and decode disaggregation](#prefill-and-decode-disaggregation) section counts only requests that meet every SLO, and maximizing throughput can lower it. The 7B at 2,048 tokens of context fills memory at 54 sequences, where a step takes 21.3 ms and yields 2,532 tokens per second. A TPOT target of 15 ms allows at most 34 sequences, whose step takes 14.9 ms and yields 2,279 tokens per second. Packing to 54 raises throughput by 11%, and every request then misses the target, so goodput falls to zero.

Capacity planning turns demand and targets into a GPU count. **Little's law** is the core tool. It says the average number of requests in a system equals their arrival rate times the average time each spends there. Decode concurrency is therefore request rate times reply length times TPOT.

The law makes no assumption about how requests arrive or how long each one stays, only that the system keeps up on average. That is why it suits bursty traffic and replies of every length. For decode, the time a request spends in the system is its reply length times TPOT, because it receives one token per step. At a TPOT of 23 ms, a 500-token reply keeps its request decoding for $500 \times 0.023 = 11.5$ seconds, so at 50 arrivals a second about $50 \times 11.5 = 575$ sequences are decoding across the fleet at any moment. That count is the batch the fleet must hold, and it fixes both the cache memory and the bytes every step reads.

The relationship runs in a circle. TPOT decides how many sequences are in flight, the number in flight decides how much cache each step reads, and the bytes read decide TPOT. A plan therefore guesses a TPOT, derives the batch, computes the step time and adjusts until the guess and the result agree. Prefill enters the same loop. With chunked prefill, prompts share each replica's iterations, so decode only gets the time that prefill leaves over. If prefill fills 40% of a replica's time, every stream's gap between tokens stretches by $1/0.6 = 1.67$ times. The prompt load sets that fraction directly, so a plan sizes prefill first and then checks whether decode still meets its target in the time that remains.

Step 6 holds the main lesson. TPOT grows like $1/(1 - f)$, where $f$ is the fraction of time spent on prefill. The last few points of utilization are therefore the most expensive, and capacity has to be planned for the peak rather than the average. The plan also shows what each earlier section is worth. A 50% prefix-cache hit rate halves the prefill demand, which is most of this workload's cost. FP8 doubles both the prefill rate and the cache room. Disaggregation would separate the two numbers, sizing about 3 prefill replicas and 1 decode replica for the average load.

Reasoning models move the cost to the other phase. With 10 requests per second, 1,000-token prompts and 3,000-token replies, prefill needs only 0.44 of a replica. But Little's law puts hundreds of sequences in flight with contexts averaging 2,500 tokens, which is 820 MB of cache each. At 2 replicas the cache would need 60.7 GB per GPU, more than the 54.76 GB available. The plan therefore fails on memory. Three replicas run 151 sequences each at a TPOT of 15 ms. That is 24 GPUs for one fifth of the chat workload's request rate, and the binding limits are cache capacity and decode bandwidth rather than prefill compute. This is why FP8 caches, grouped or latent attention, and sparse attention matter most for reasoning traffic. The 70B's 4,096-token context caps these replies, and longer reasoning traces make the shift sharper.

## Serving diagnosis

The [capacity planning](#capacity-planning) section sizes a fleet before launch. Once it runs, the symptoms point back to specific sections, and the table maps each symptom to what to measure and the usual fix. [The Fundamentals diagnosis table](/tutorials/llm-fundamentals/inference-and-decoding/#latency-diagnosis) covers the first-line cases. These rows assume continuous batching and paging are already in place.

| Symptom | Likely cause | What to measure | Usual fix |
|---|---|---|---|
| TTFT rises steeply with load while TPOT holds | Prefill takes most of each replica's time | Fraction of iteration time spent on prefill tokens | Prefix caching, FP8, more replicas or a separate prefill pool |
| TPOT spikes whenever long prompts arrive | Chunks too large or no chunking | Iteration time against prompt tokens per iteration | Chunked prefill with a smaller token budget |
| TPOT grows with context even at modest batch | Cache reads dominate each step | Cache bytes against weight bytes per step | FP8 cache, a grouped or latent attention model, a context cap |
| GPU reports full utilization but throughput is flat | Decode is bandwidth bound | Achieved bandwidth against 3.35 TB/s | Larger batches, quantized weights and cache |
| Frequent preemption or recompute storms | Admitted work exceeds cache capacity | Free blocks and preemptions per minute | Admission control, lower per-request token limits, wider tensor parallelism |
| Low prefix-cache hit rate | Dynamic text early in prompts or cache-blind routing | Hit rate per template and per replica | Static text first, prefix-aware routing, a CPU or SSD cache tier |
| Speculation slows serving under load | Verification turns the step compute bound | Speedup against batch size | Fewer drafts or no speculation above a batch threshold |
| Quantized model regresses on long or code tasks | Outlier channels or cache quantization error | Per-task scores against bf16 | Smoothing, per-channel key scales, higher precision for sensitive layers |
| Tensor parallelism stops scaling past one node | All-reduces over InfiniBand | All-reduce time per step | Tensor parallelism inside nodes, pipeline stages or replicas across them |

Each row starts from what an operator sees and ends at a change to the system, and the middle columns keep the two apart. Several rows look alike on the surface. Slower tokens can come from oversized prefill chunks, from long contexts filling each step with cache reads, or from speculation turning the step compute bound, and the three fixes have nothing in common. The measurement column separates them. TPOT that jumps exactly when a long prompt joins the batch points to the scheduler. TPOT that climbs steadily as conversations grow points to the cache. TPOT that worsens with batch size only while speculation is on points to the drafts. Each likely cause is the subject of an earlier section, so a confirmed row leads back to the section that prices its fix.

The 70B fleet from the [capacity planning](#capacity-planning) section shows one row in use. After launch the five replicas meet the 30 ms TPOT target on average, but streaming users report pauses, and the 99th-percentile gap between tokens reads about 50 ms. The first measurement is iteration time against the prompt tokens in each iteration. Iterations with no prompt tokens take about 10 ms, while iterations that carry a whole 1,000-token prompt take about 50 ms, because at width 8 and 40% of peak each prompt token adds about 44 microseconds of arithmetic to a replica's iteration. That is the second row. The token budget was set high enough to admit a whole prompt at once, so no chunking happened. A budget of 512 tokens caps each iteration at about 22 ms of arithmetic plus the all-reduces. That is about 25 ms in all, under the target. Each prompt now finishes over three iterations instead of one, which adds about 25 ms to a TTFT target of a full second.

The table works only with measurements split by phase and by percentile. A single average latency hides almost every row.
