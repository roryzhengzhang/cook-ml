Consider a fictional finance adaptation run on the [reference 7B architecture](/tutorials/llm-deep-dive/#scale-reference-models). After 20 billion tokens of filings and earnings-call transcripts, held-out loss falls from 1.93 to 1.65 nats per token. After the same fine-tuning recipe, exact extraction accuracy falls from 71% to 68%. The constructed numbers illustrate why improvements in predicting filing text can coexist with worse performance on a particular task. The failure audit and later fine-tuning scores are parts of the same worked case.

Adapting a general model to a specialist field is **domain adaptation**, and it spans everything from a better prompt to a new training run. The methods repair different things. Continued training on raw filings teaches the language of the field. Fine-tuning on labeled examples teaches a task's behavior. Retrieval supplies facts that change every quarter. Choosing a method before knowing which of the three is missing is how 20 billion tokens get spent on the wrong problem, so the figure starts from 60 wrong answers and sorts them by the cheapest test that fixes each one.

![Diagnosis map](/images/llm-deep-dive/16g-diagnosis-map.png)

In this sample, missing facts outnumber missing language by more than three to one, 26 failures against 8. Every repair in the audit was a change to the prompt, and 48 of the 60 failures yielded to one. That turns the question from whether a fix exists into whether it scales. Retrieved evidence scales well, since the index can take each quarter's filings without touching the weights. Demonstrations work too, and fine-tuning on labeled examples carries the same behavior further than three examples in a prompt can. The worked example in the [fine-tuning plan](#fine-tuning-plan) section uses a nine-point gain in exact match. Definitions scale worst, because a product meets thousands of domain terms and cannot paste a glossary into every request. Only that case argues for training on domain text, and here it covers 8 of the 60.

The chapter follows that sorting. It opens with the diagnosis and with the kinds of shift that separate financial text from what a general model read, because each kind calls for a different fix and only the audit tells them apart. Training on domain text comes next, since it is the most expensive fix and often the first one chosen. Continued pretraining needs a rewarmed learning rate to move the weights at all, new tokenizer entries make domain terms cheaper to read, and replay limits what the run forgets. The section on slice loss then resolves the puzzle above, where held-out loss falls while the task gets worse. Its answer is that the numbers a task needs appear once in raw text and cannot be learned in advance, and that points to the two alternatives that follow. Synthetic data makes domain text teach more per token, and retrieval or long context leaves the weights alone. The fine-tuning half starts with an experiment plan, packs examples so they cannot read each other, and evaluates under the time and company shifts where most leaks hide, since a gain measured on a leaky split is not a gain. A last section turns the results into release gates and a transfer decision.

## Failure diagnosis
Three causes often produce similar wrong answers in finance. The model may misread **domain language**, such as taking “material weakness” as an ordinary flaw. It may recognize the relevant figures and get the **task behavior** wrong, such as returning net debt when the product asked for total debt. Or the input may omit **fresh facts** that came out after training. Other causes, including extraction errors, multi-step reasoning failures and bad labels, can look similar.

A **failure audit** separates them. It samples wrong answers and records for each one the question, the evidence that was available, the required answer and the model's answer. Here the sample is 60 of the 200 finance questions the unchanged 7B got wrong with a plain prompt. Each failure then gets a **controlled intervention**, a rerun with exactly one thing added to the prompt. The order tests inexpensive ways to change the input before considering a new training run.

1. Supply the gold evidence, the filing passage that contains the answer. Twenty-six become correct. Those answers benefit from explicit evidence in the prompt.
2. For the remaining 34, add three worked demonstrations of the required output. Fourteen more become correct. Those answers benefit from examples of the requested format, fields or conventions.
3. For the remaining 20, add a one-line definition of the domain term involved. Eight more become correct.
4. Twelve stay wrong. These cases need inspection. A bad label and a failure to combine two tables are two possible explanations.

Adding one thing per rerun shows which intervention helped. The result can narrow the diagnosis, since several mechanisms may respond to the same intervention. If evidence, demonstrations and a definition went in together and a failure disappeared, nothing would say which of the three had been missing. The order matters too, because a failure is credited to the first intervention that repairs it, and some failures would yield to more than one. Putting evidence first sends those shared cases to retrieval, the cheapest fix and the only one that keeps working after the next quarter's filings. Fine-tuning and continued pretraining are credited only with failures the cheaper route left unrepaired, so the counts are conservative about how much training the product needs. The twelve that nothing repairs are read by hand before any decision, since a wrong gold label sends a project after an error the model never made.

The counts set the order of work. Retrieval addresses 43 percent of these failures, [supervised fine-tuning](/tutorials/llm-fundamentals/post-training-from-base-model-to-assistant/#supervised-fine-tuning) another 23 percent, and only 13 percent point at the language itself. A prompt can define a handful of terms, but a product that meets thousands of them cannot paste a glossary into every request. Two thousand one-line definitions of about 20 tokens each would add 40,000 tokens to every prompt, sixteen times the 2,500 tokens of retrieved evidence that the [retrieval versus training](#retrieval-versus-training) section prices. At that scale continued pretraining becomes a candidate. The best prompt found here is retrieved evidence plus three demonstrations. The unchanged model with that prompt becomes the prompt baseline that every later method has to beat on the same questions. Without it, a cleaner dataset or a better prompt written during the project can be mistaken for a training gain.

## Domain shift
Financial text differs from the text a general model trained on in several separate ways, and each kind of difference calls for its own fix. A **domain shift** is any change between the distribution a model learned from and the one it is used on. Four kinds come up in finance, plus a fifth that is about the task rather than the text.

**Covariate shift** changes which inputs appear while the right output for each input stays the same. Filings use long tables, legal phrasing and many numbers. Measured with the Llama 2 tokenizer that the reference model uses, 120-word chunks of eight recent 10-K filings average 1.75 tokens per word against 1.47 for chat replies, and 16 percent of all filing tokens are single digits against about 2 percent in the chat text. **Label shift** changes how often each class occurs while keeping the distribution of inputs within each class fixed. The mix of inputs overall can change as a result. A classifier of earnings-call guidance might be trained in a calm year, when perhaps 15 percent of companies cut guidance. If the language of calls within each guidance class stays similar, changing the fraction of calls with cuts from 15% to 42% gives an example of pure label shift. **Concept shift** changes the mapping from input to answer. Loughran and McDonald found that almost three fourths of the words the Harvard dictionary marks as negative are not negative in financial text. Words such as "liability", "tax" and "cost" are neutral there, so a sentiment model carried over from general text misreads a neutral 10-K as gloomy.

**Temporal shift** changes facts and conventions over time. The leasing standard ASC 842 moved operating leases onto the balance sheet, so a retailer's reported liabilities jumped with no change in its business. It took effect for public companies in fiscal years beginning after December 15, 2018. Every quarter also replaces the latest reported figures of every listed company. **Task shift** keeps the text and changes the job, as when the same filing must now yield five extracted fields instead of a next token.

![Domain shift kinds](/images/llm-deep-dive/16g-domain-shift-kinds.png)

Each kind points at a different remedy, because each changes a different part of the problem. Under pure covariate shift, the answer distribution for a given input stays the same. Reweighting or collecting more examples from the target domain can help, especially where the original training set had little coverage. Continued pretraining shows the model filing-style text, and tokenizer extension makes that text cheaper to read. Under pure label shift, correcting the class priors can update predicted probabilities if the new rates can be estimated and the inputs within each class remain comparable. A downturn can also change how companies describe their results, in which case the prior correction addresses only part of the shift. Concept shift is the one that unlabeled text cannot fix. Reading more filings shows the model how "liability" is used, but never says that a sentence containing it is not gloomy. Only labeled examples carry that, and the same holds for task shift. Temporal shift breaks facts rather than skills. In the illustrative temporal panel, an answer from the weights alone loses most of its accuracy within one quarter of the training cutoff, while an answer read from a retrieved filing holds steady. Retrieval and date-split evaluation therefore address it. Real data usually mixes several kinds, which is why the audit in the [failure diagnosis](#failure-diagnosis) section comes first.

Under pure covariate shift, a model trained on source data can be pointed at the target by weighting each source example by how much more common its input is in the target. This is **importance weighting**, with weight $w(x) = P_t(x)/P_s(x)$, where $P_s(x)$ and $P_t(x)$ are how often input $x$ occurs in the source and in the target. Suppose a source set of 1,000 examples is 90 percent general text and 10 percent filing-style text, while the target is half and half. The general examples get weight $0.5/0.9 = 0.556$ and the filing examples get $0.5/0.1 = 5$. The weights sum to $900 \times 0.556 + 100 \times 5 = 1{,}000$, so the average is unbiased, but the estimate now leans on only 100 examples.

The **effective sample size** $(\sum w)^2/\sum w^2$ measures how many unweighted examples the weighted set is worth:

$$
\frac{1{,}000^2}{900 \times 0.556^2 + 100 \times 5^2} = \frac{1{,}000{,}000}{277.8 + 2{,}500} = 360.
$$

The 1,000 weighted examples carry the information of 360. For the text an LLM reads, the density ratio cannot be estimated reliably anyway, and no weighting creates inputs the source never contained or repairs a changed meaning. Reweighting fixes frequencies, and only target examples fix concepts.

</details>

## Continued pretraining
A model learns a field's language the same way it learned general language, by predicting the next token of a great deal of that field's text. **Domain-adaptive pretraining**, or DAPT, continues the pretraining loss on an unlabeled domain corpus, starting from the released weights. [Don't Stop Pretraining](https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.10964) showed the effect across biomedical papers, computer science papers, news and reviews. It also added **task-adaptive pretraining**, or TAPT. This is a further short stage on the unlabeled inputs of the task itself, such as the management discussion and notes sections that an extraction product actually reads. In their experiments DAPT followed by TAPT beat either one alone.

The running corpus holds 8B unique tokens of filings and 2B of call transcripts, after removing [near-duplicate](/tutorials/llm-fundamentals/pretraining-at-scale/#deduplication-and-quality-filtering) boilerplate and holding out whole document families and later dates for evaluation. A 20B-token run is 1 percent of the base model's 2T pretraining tokens. Its compute is

$$
6ND = 6 \times 6.74 \times 10^9 \times 2 \times 10^{10} = 8.09 \times 10^{20} \text{ FLOPs},
$$

which is about 568 GPU-hours at 40 percent of an H100's 989 TFLOP/s, or three days on one 8-GPU node. Full updates need 107.8 GB of [AdamW state](/tutorials/llm-fundamentals/pretraining-at-scale/#optimizer-memory), which is more than one 80 GB GPU holds. The state is therefore sharded across the node as [Parallelism](/tutorials/llm-deep-dive/distributed-training-and-parallelism/) describes.

The learning rate is the setting that most separates continued pretraining from fine-tuning. The base run ended its [cosine schedule](/tutorials/llm-fundamentals/pretraining-at-scale/#learning-rate-schedules) at a floor of $3 \times 10^{-5}$, and training on at that floor moves the weights so slowly that 20B tokens change little. **Learning rate rewarming** raises the rate again over the first 100 steps and then decays it along a new cosine. Here the rate rises to $1.5 \times 10^{-4}$, half the original peak, and decays to $1.5 \times 10^{-5}$. Each batch holds 512 windows of 4,096 tokens, or 2,097,152 tokens per step, so the run takes 9,537 steps.

![Rewarm and held out loss](/images/llm-deep-dive/16g-rewarm-and-held-out-loss.png)

Rewarming trades a short setback for the ability to move at all. At its peak of $1.5 \times 10^{-4}$, each AdamW step moves the weights about five times as far as a step at the old floor of $3 \times 10^{-5}$. Right after the rewarm both held-out losses rise for a short stretch, since the larger steps first knock the weights out of the minimum the base run settled into. Over the run the **held-out domain loss** falls from 1.928 to 1.650 nats per token. That is the loss on filings from document families and dates the run never sees. General text pays for the gain. Its held-out loss starts near 1.80 and creeps up to 1.88 without replay, and to 1.82 with the 25 percent replay that the [catastrophic forgetting](#catastrophic-forgetting) section works out. Replay therefore removes about three quarters of the damage, and in the sweep of the [catastrophic forgetting](#catastrophic-forgetting) section it costs only 0.015 nats of the domain gain. A higher peak learns the domain faster and forgets more, so the peak is a dial between the two curves.

[Gupta et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.04014) continued Pythia 410M models trained on the 300B-token Pile with 297B tokens of SlimPajama. Rewarming first raised the loss on both the old and the new data, and over the longer run improved the new-data loss beyond a model trained from scratch on the new data alone. [Ibrahim et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.08763) then showed that the combination of rewarming, re-decaying and replaying part of the previous data matched retraining from scratch on all the data, at a fraction of the compute. They tested a 405M model on a weak English-to-English shift and a stronger English-to-German shift, and a 10B model on the weak shift. They also proposed schedules that are not tied to a fixed token budget, so a run can keep absorbing new data without planning its end in advance. These schedules are close in spirit to the [warmup stable decay](/tutorials/llm-fundamentals/pretraining-at-scale/#learning-rate-schedules) shape.

</details>

A team that owns the pretraining run puts domain data in the decay phase instead, the [mid-training](/tutorials/llm-fundamentals/pretraining-at-scale/#mid-training) stage where the last batches shape the weights most. OLMo 2's 7B model, for example, read 50B tokens of its curated Dolmino Mix 1124 three times in different orders while the learning rate fell to zero, then averaged the three results ([OLMo 2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.00656)). The decay phase also tests data cheaply. [Llama 3](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.21783) judged a candidate dataset by decaying a half-trained 8B model over 40B tokens with 30 percent of the mix drawn from the candidate. The finance version decays the reference model over 2B tokens twice and compares slice losses and task scores. One run uses 30 percent candidate data, such as a new set of transcripts, and the other uses 30 percent existing domain data. Each costs about 57 GPU-hours.

The starting checkpoint matters too. [Cheng et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.09530) found that continued pretraining on raw biomedicine, finance and law corpora added domain knowledge but sharply hurt the models' ability to answer questions from prompts. Continued pretraining therefore starts from the base model and is followed by fine-tuning, or mixes instruction data into the domain stream, rather than running raw filings through a finished chat model.

## Tokenizer extension
A tokenizer built on general text cuts frequent domain words into pieces, and every piece costs a position in the window and a decode step. The Llama 2 tokenizer splits "cash" into "▁c ash", "debt" into "▁deb t", "securities" into "▁se cur ities" and "EBITDA" into "▁E BIT DA". Across the eight 10-K filings its [fertility](/tutorials/llm-fundamentals/tokenization-and-embeddings/#fertility) is 1.76 tokens per word. **Tokenizer extension** adds whole domain terms to the vocabulary as new entries, each with a new row in the embedding table and a new row in the output head.

The gain is measurable before any training. Words were scored on six of the filings by how often they occur times how many pieces they would save, and the top 2,000 multi-piece words were added. On the two held-out filings from Exxon and Walmart, the token count fell by 9.94 percent and fertility from 1.829 to 1.648. Adding 100 words saved 2.9 percent and 8,000 saved 12.3 percent. On the six filings used to pick the words, 2,000 saved 13.1 percent, which overstates what new text gains. Ten percent fewer tokens means ten percent less compute per filing in training and in serving, and ten percent more filing in each 4,096-token window.

The costs come in three forms. The 2,000 new rows add $2{,}000 \times 4{,}096 \times 2 = 16{,}384{,}000$ parameters, 0.24 percent of the model, all starting untrained. Every checkpoint, adapter and serving artifact must now ship with the new tokenizer, so older adapters stop working. And per-token loss is no longer comparable before and after the change, since the same text has fewer tokens. Comparisons across the change are therefore made in bits per byte, as the deep dive under [perplexity](/tutorials/llm-fundamentals/language-modeling-and-next-token-prediction/#perplexity) explains.

A modern base often makes the question moot. Qwen3's 151,669-entry tokenizer keeps "cash", "debt", "goodwill", "impairment" and "securities" whole. On the same eight filings it needs 1.53 tokens per word, 13 percent fewer than the Llama 2 tokenizer. It still cuts "EBITDA" into three pieces. Extension pays most for an old tokenizer, a field with an unusual script or notation, and a corpus large enough to train thousands of new rows.

## Catastrophic forgetting
Training on filings alone pulls every weight toward filings, and abilities the filings never exercise decay. This is the [catastrophic forgetting](/tutorials/llm-fundamentals/post-training-from-base-model-to-assistant/#supervised-fine-tuning) that general held-out loss rising to 1.88 in the [continued pretraining](#continued-pretraining) section shows. **Replay** mixes general text back into the stream, and the **replay ratio** $r$ is the share of training tokens it takes. Each source then has an exposure, the number of times its unique tokens are read:

$$
\text{epochs}_s = \frac{\text{share}_s \times D}{U_s},
$$

where $D$ is the run's total tokens, $\text{share}_s$ is the fraction of them drawn from source $s$, and $U_s$ is the number of unique tokens that source holds. [Data mixtures](/tutorials/llm-fundamentals/pretraining-at-scale/#data-mixtures) in pretraining follow the same bookkeeping. The unit of sampling matters here. Measured over the eight filings, a 10-K averages 137,733 tokens with the Llama 2 tokenizer. A call transcript runs perhaps 10,000, so mixing the two half and half by document gives filings 93 percent of the tokens.

Forgetting happens because nothing in a filings-only objective rewards the old abilities. Every step lowers the loss on filings, and a weight that also served code or conversation moves whenever moving it helps the filings, with nothing pulling it back. Replay restores that pull. While general text stays in the stream, an update that damages general ability raises part of the loss being minimized, so the optimizer favors updates that help the filings without undoing what the general text needs. The replay does not have to teach anything new, so even a small share can cut forgetting sharply.

The exposure count is the other half of the bookkeeping. A source read less than once is partly unseen, and one read many times starts to be memorized. [Muennighoff et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.16264) found that with fixed compute, up to about four epochs of repeated data change the loss very little compared with unique data, and that further repetition is worth less and less. A small source such as the call transcripts can therefore be read more often than its share of the corpus would suggest, within that limit. The price of replay is compute. A replay token teaches nothing about the domain, so at a fixed budget replay thins the domain exposure, and at a fixed domain exposure it lengthens the run.

| What trains | Trained parameters | Weights and optimizer state |
|---|---|---|

## Slice loss
Held-out loss is an average over every token of the held-out text, and a task depends on a small, specific subset of those tokens. The opening's puzzle comes from that gap. A **slice loss** is the held-out loss computed over one class of tokens, such as digits, and it shows where an average gain came from.

The average weights each kind of token by how often it appears in the text, while the task cares about the tokens its answers are made of. An extraction answer is a few digits, a scale word such as "million" and a period such as "three months ended June 30", and together these are a small share of a filing. The useful slices therefore follow the task's answers and its error types. Digits track the amounts, scale words track scale and unit errors, and dates and period phrases track wrong-period answers. When one slice improves and another gets worse, the average reports only their sum weighted by share, and the walkthrough below shows a case where that sum points the wrong way.

Such a split is likely after continued pretraining. Text that repeats across many documents gets easier with every pass, while the numbers in a held-out document are new and cannot be learned in advance, and extraction answers are made almost entirely of those numbers. Tracking slices costs almost nothing, since the loss of every held-out token is already computed for the average. A slice is the same numbers grouped by a label on each token. A slice is still a proxy, though, and the decisive check is a short fine-tune of a checkpoint followed by a task score. As the [fine-tuning plan](#fine-tuning-plan) section works out, that fine-tune costs about half a GPU-hour. Scoring checkpoints at 5B, 10B, 15B and 20B tokens therefore adds about 2 GPU-hours to a 568 GPU-hour run, under half a percent. The base model is scored the same way, so a run that only hurts the task leaves the base as the best candidate.

The mechanism is plausible from what the corpus contains. Boilerplate, legal phrasing and section headings repeat across thousands of filings and are easy to learn. The digits in a held-out filing are new numbers. A 10-K's comparative columns repeat the prior year's figures, so a model that has read last year's 10-K now leans toward stale values and toward the wrong column. That is exactly the wrong-period error. In the worked example, scale and format errors fall while wrong-period errors rise by more.

This is **negative transfer**, an adaptation that leaves the target task worse than the baseline. It differs from forgetting, which is damage to abilities outside the target. Three habits catch it early. Slice losses are tracked for the token classes the task depends on, here digits by period. Every 5B tokens, a checkpoint gets a short fine-tune and a task score, and the checkpoint is chosen by that score rather than by the lowest loss. And because numbers change every quarter, the [retrieval versus training](#retrieval-versus-training) section leaves them to retrieval, so the weights are trained on the language around them.

In this run the base plus fine-tuning scores 710 of 1,000 and the 20B checkpoint plus fine-tuning scores 680, so selection by task score keeps the base. Splitting the digits by period sharpens the first habit. A filing's tables print the current period next to the prior one, so each digit can be labeled by the column it sits in. If the stale-value mechanism holds, loss on current-period digits rises while loss on prior-period digits falls, and a slice that shows that split points at the cause before any fine-tune runs.

## Synthetic data
Raw domain text teaches a fact slowly, because a model needs to see a fact many times and in many phrasings to recall it, and a filing states each figure once. Synthetic data uses a stronger model to rewrite or expand the corpus into forms that teach more per token.

**Rephrasing** rewrites existing documents in other styles and trains on the originals plus the rewrites. [WRAP](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.16380) had an instruction-tuned model rewrite web pages "like Wikipedia" or as question-answer pairs. On the C4 web corpus it reached the same quality about three times faster. Its perplexity was also more than 10 percent lower at equal compute across subsets of the Pile. The domain version rewrites dense filing passages as plain explanations or as questions and answers. **Reading-comprehension conversion** appends tasks about each document to the document itself. [Cheng et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.09530) did this for biomedicine, finance and law, and it improved prompting performance across tasks in all three, where raw domain text had damaged it. They report that their 7B model became competitive with much larger domain models such as the 50B-parameter BloombergGPT.

**Synthetic question answering** asks a strong model to write question-answer pairs from each training document, then keeps only the pairs that pass a check. For extraction the check is mechanical. Every number in the answer must appear in the source passage, with its unit and period. Fifty pairs from each of 10,000 training filings make 500,000 candidates. If the number check rejects 18 percent, 410,000 remain. At 150 tokens each that is about 60M tokens, small next to the corpus and dense in exactly the behavior the task needs.

**Synthetic continued pretraining** goes furthest. [EntiGraph](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.07431) extracted the entities from 265 books totaling 1.3M tokens and had GPT-4 Turbo write 455M tokens of text relating pairs and triples of them, about 350 tokens for every original one. Continued pretraining of Llama 3 8B on that corpus raised closed-book accuracy on questions about the books from 39.49 to 56.22 percent, rising with the logarithm of the synthetic token count. Retrieval alone reached 60.35 percent, and the two together 62.60 percent. Simple paraphrasing scaled much worse, and training on the 1.3M raw tokens did worse than the untouched base. Distillation of domain behavior is the fine-tuning counterpart, covered in [post-training](/tutorials/llm-fundamentals/post-training-from-base-model-to-assistant/#distillation). In it, a stronger model writes the answers a smaller one learns to copy. The student copies the teacher's errors along with its style, and the teacher's terms of use may restrict the practice.

The risks come from the generator. If it is shown test filings, or questions close to the test set, the synthetic data carries the answers into training, a form of the [contamination](/tutorials/llm-fundamentals/evaluation-reliability-and-safety/#benchmark-contamination) that inflates scores. Generation therefore runs only on training-split documents, and the synthetic output gets the same n-gram overlap check against test items as any other training text. The second risk is **homogenization**. Every rewrite carries one generator's phrasing, and [Shumailov et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.17493) showed that models trained repeatedly on generated data lose the tails of the original distribution, which they called model collapse. Keeping the real text in the mix, varying prompts and generators, and capping the synthetic share keep rare cases from washing out. In finance those are the cases that matter most, such as restatements and unusual accounting.

## Retrieval versus training
A figure stored in the weights goes stale the day a newer filing appears, and in finance that happens on a schedule. Large accelerated filers submit a 10-Q within 40 days of each quarter's end and a 10-K within 60 days of the year's end, so every quarter replaces the latest figures of every listed company. Three routes can supply those facts. The weights can hold them, answering closed-book. [Retrieval](/tutorials/llm-fundamentals/using-llms-in-applications/#retrieval-augmented-generation) can find the passage and put it in the prompt. Or a long context window can hold the whole filing.

Retrieval wins on freshness and cost for most questions. Five chunks of 500 tokens add 2,500 prompt tokens, and prefill on the 7B costs $2N$ FLOPs per token, $3.4 \times 10^{13}$ FLOPs per request. At 100,000 requests a day that is about 2.4 GPU-hours a day, or 864 a year. Rerunning the 20B-token continued pretraining every quarter costs $4 \times 568 = 2{,}271$ GPU-hours a year, and the closed-book answers still lag by the retraining time and cannot cite the page they came from.

Long context is now a real option rather than a thought experiment. [Llama 3](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.21783) extended its window to 128K tokens in six stages over about 800B tokens. [Qwen3](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.09388) trains at 32,768 tokens and lists its models from 4B parameters up at 128K, reached with YaRN position scaling at inference. A whole 10-K still does not always fit. The eight filings average 137,733 tokens with the Llama 2 tokenizer and 119,840 with Qwen3's. They range from 53,408 for Apple's to 295,886 for JPMorgan's, and three of the eight exceed 131,072. The cost is large too. Llama 3 70B shares the reference 70B's shape apart from its larger vocabulary and position settings. For a 70B model of that shape, a 131,072-token prompt holds 40 GiB of KV cache and needs about

$$
2 \times 6.90 \times 10^{10} \times 131{,}072 + 2 \times 131{,}072^2 \times 8{,}192 \times 80 = 1.8 \times 10^{16} + 2.3 \times 10^{16} = 4.1 \times 10^{16}
$$

FLOPs of prefill, where the first term is the weights and the second is attention across 80 layers of width 8,192. At 40 percent utilization that is about 103 GPU-seconds, roughly 13 seconds to the first token on an 8-GPU node. The same model answering from 2,500 retrieved tokens needs 781 MiB of cache and about 115 times less compute. [Xu et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.03025) found that a 4K-token window with retrieval matched a 16K-token window extended by fine-tuning, and that retrieval improved Llama 2 70B at every window size they tried. [Long Context](/tutorials/llm-deep-dive/long-context-and-document-models/) covers how long windows are built and served.

| Route | Freshness | Citation | Cost per request | Cost to update |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Facts in the weights | As of the last training run | None | Lowest | A training run |
| Retrieval | As of the last index update | Passage and page | 2,500 extra prompt tokens | Index new filings in minutes |
| Whole filing in a long window | As of the filing supplied | Page, if the model is asked | Up to 131,072 prompt tokens | None |

Most production systems split the work. Retrieval supplies the figures and their sources, long context handles the few questions that genuinely need a whole document, such as a summary of all risk factors, and training is spent on reading and behavior rather than on the numbers. [RAFT](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.10131) makes the split explicit by fine-tuning on questions paired with retrieved documents that include distractors, so the training teaches the model to use evidence instead of memorizing it.

## Fine-tuning plan
Before training a debt extractor, the team has to agree on what counts as debt and how it will tell whether the model improved. A short training run can still give misleading results if those decisions are left until afterward. A **fine-tuning plan** defines the task, labeling rules, data splits, baseline, success metric, and stopping rule before training starts. The table fills it in for the debt-extraction task. For a question about a company's debt, that task returns five fields, which are the amount, currency, scale, period and source span.

| Part of the plan | Debt-extraction entry |
|---|---|
| Task definition | Total debt excludes operating lease liabilities. The period is the balance-sheet date. "Insufficient evidence" is a valid answer. |
| Data quality | Two annotators per item, a written rubric of scoring rules, disagreements resolved by revising the rubric |
| Splits | 6,000 training examples from 1,500 filings, 1,000 development questions from 250 later filings, and a 400-question test set split by date |
| Baseline | The unchanged 7B with retrieved evidence and three demonstrations, 62.0 percent exact match |
| Metric | All five fields exact after normalizing units, so 12.4 million equals 12,400,000 |
| Stopping rule | Evaluation every 25 steps. Training stops after three evaluations without a 0.5-point gain, or at three epochs, and the best checkpoint is kept. |

The data-quality row is where the leasing standard from the [domain shift](#domain-shift) section resurfaces. Two annotators labeled the same 300 items and agreed on 252, an **inter-annotator agreement** of 84 percent. Of the 48 disagreements, 30 were about whether operating lease liabilities count as debt, which is a gap in the definition rather than an annotator error. Adding one line to the rubric raised agreement to 94 percent on a fresh batch of 100. A model trained on the first version would have learned a coin flip on leases.

The compute is small. The examples average 1,000 tokens, prompt and retrieved evidence included. An epoch is therefore 6M tokens and three epochs 18M. At $6N$ FLOPs per token the run costs $7.3 \times 10^{17}$ FLOPs, about half a GPU-hour. Packed into 4,096-token windows with 16 windows to a batch, an epoch is 92 steps, so the **stopping rule** gets 11 evaluations over the run. The loss covers only answer tokens, using the [prompt loss masking](/tutorials/llm-fundamentals/post-training-from-base-model-to-assistant/#prompt-loss-masking) from post-training. In this hypothetical run, the fine-tuned model reaches 71.0 percent exact match on the development set, nine points above the baseline. If its 1,000 answers were independent, the 95 percent interval would be

$$
\pm 1.96\sqrt{\frac{0.71 \times 0.29}{1{,}000}} = \pm 2.8 \text{ points},
$$

and the [time-split evaluation](#time-split-evaluation) section shows why they are not.

Analysts also have preferences about how answers are written. They may prefer replies that state the period first, cite the page and flag estimates, and many differently worded replies satisfy that. Those requirements suit [direct preference optimization](/tutorials/llm-fundamentals/post-training-from-base-model-to-assistant/#direct-preference-optimization) from the fine-tuned model as the reference, with perhaps 2,000 pairs in which analysts picked the better of two replies. The risk is that raters reward polished, confident text, including confident wrong numbers. The plan evaluates exact match separately on the preference-tuned model. It allows a drop of at most one percentage point.

## Document masking
Fine-tuning examples here run from 600 to 1,400 tokens and training windows hold 4,096, so [packing](/tutorials/llm-fundamentals/pretraining-at-scale/#sequence-packing) several examples into one window saves most of the padding. An average example of 1,000 tokens fills 24 percent of a window, so one example per window would leave about three quarters of every window as padding. The 6,000 training examples at 16 windows per batch would then take 375 steps per epoch instead of the 92 that the [fine-tuning plan](#fine-tuning-plan) section counts with packing, four times the compute for the same data. Pretraining already uses a document mask so packed documents cannot read each other. Fine-tuning raises the stakes, because the packed neighbors are answered examples. In pretraining a neighbor is usually unrelated text. Here it is another question of the same kind, with an amount, a scale and a period written out in exactly the form the model is learning to produce. In the figure's toy window, a 3-token example A and a 5-token example B share 8 positions.

![Packing masks](/images/llm-deep-dive/16g-packing-masks.png)

With a plain causal mask, 15 of the 36 attention pairs let tokens of B read A. In an extraction dataset, A's evidence and answer sit right there, including its 412 million dollar figure. The model can learn to copy amounts from unrelated earlier text, a shortcut that does not exist at serving time when each request arrives alone. The document mask on the right blocks those pairs and leaves 21, all inside one example. Position reset restarts the position ids at 0 for each example, so B's tokens carry the same positions they would carry at serving. With rotary embeddings the scores inside one example depend only on relative distance, so once the mask isolates the examples the offset changes little. Models with learned absolute positions do need the reset, and it keeps the inputs identical to serving either way.

A full window shows the scale. Four examples of 1,400, 1,100, 900 and 600 tokens fill 4,000 positions. A causal mask over them allows $4{,}000 \times 4{,}001/2 = 8{,}002{,}000$ pairs, while the four separate examples need only

$$
980{,}700 + 605{,}550 + 405{,}450 + 180{,}300 = 2{,}172{,}000.
$$

So 72.9 percent of the pairs a plain mask computes cross between examples. **Variable-length attention** kernels take the example boundaries as input and skip the blocked pairs entirely, so the mask saves compute rather than costing it. FlashAttention's variable-length interface is one such kernel. Attention is about 7 percent of the forward pass at this length, so the saving is a few percent of the step.

Packing changes how many scored tokens land in each micro-batch, and averaging the loss per micro-batch then weights examples unevenly. Suppose one micro-batch holds 30 answer tokens with a mean loss of 0.5. The next micro-batch is accumulated into the same optimizer step and holds 3 answer tokens with a mean loss of 2.0. Averaging the two means gives

$$
\frac{0.5 + 2.0}{2} = 1.25, \qquad \text{while the mean over all scored tokens is} \qquad \frac{30 \times 0.5 + 3 \times 2.0}{33} = 0.636.
$$

The first version gives the 3-token micro-batch half the weight of the step, against its $3/33 = 0.09$ share of the scored tokens, an overweight of 5.5 times. The fix is to sum the token losses across all micro-batches and devices and divide once by the global count of scored tokens. Whether a long answer should count more than a short one is a separate choice between token averaging and example averaging. The plan records that choice and keeps it fixed across runs.

## Time-split evaluation
Financial test sets leak in ways a random split cannot see. Questions drawn from the same filing land on both sides of a random split, restated figures appear in later filings, and the earnings call announces a quarter's numbers before the 10-Q is filed. With 10,000 questions from 2,000 filings at five per filing, a random 80/20 split leaves a test question with at least one sibling from its filing in training with probability $1 - 0.2^4 = 0.998$. A **temporal split** trains on the past and tests on the future, and the date that matters is the **information date** rather than when the test document was filed. The information date is when a number first became public.

A leak inflates the score because a leaked question is answered from memory. If training contains a sibling question from the same filing, or a press release that states the same figure, the model can return a number it has already read, and the test then measures recall of training documents. A financial figure reaches the public several times, in the earnings release, in the 10-Q, as next year's comparative column and sometimes in a restatement. Only the earliest of these marks when the number stopped being unknown, so the cut is placed by that date and removes every document after it, whatever its type. A filing date alone would let the earlier announcements through. The price of a clean cut is data. It removes the most recent documents, which are the closest to the test period and often the most useful. The evaluated model has therefore read less recent text than the one finally shipped, and its score tends to understate the shipped model slightly. Generalization to companies the model has never read about is a separate question that a time split does not answer, and it needs its own holdout.

The rule from step 4 generalizes into an **embargo**. That is a gap between the last training date and the end of the period the test asks about, wide enough that no training document can contain the answer. Before splitting, questions are grouped by document family, so near-copies never straddle the cut. A family is the filing, its amendments, its transcript and its press release. The **entity-disjoint holdout** in step 5 answers a different question from the time split. It tests generalization to new companies rather than to new quarters. The two are reported separately because a product may need one, the other or both.

Uncertainty needs the same care. The 400 questions come from 100 companies, and answers from one company are correlated, since a model that misreads one company's table format tends to miss all four of its questions. With a within-company correlation of 0.3, the **design effect** is $1 + (4 - 1) \times 0.3 = 1.9$, so the 400 questions carry the information of about 211 independent ones. The 95 percent interval around 65.5 percent widens from $\pm 4.7$ to $\pm 6.4$ points. A **cluster bootstrap** resamples whole companies rather than single questions, which gives intervals with that correction built in. One leak cannot be closed by any split. The base model's own pretraining corpus is not public, so only test periods after the base model's training data ends rule out [contamination](/tutorials/llm-fundamentals/evaluation-reliability-and-safety/#benchmark-contamination) from that source.

## Release gates
An adapted model ships only if it beats the baseline on the target and stays inside agreed limits everywhere else, and those limits are written down before results arrive. The [release gates](/tutorials/llm-fundamentals/evaluation-reliability-and-safety/#red-teaming-and-release-gates) of the fundamentals series apply, and domain adaptation adds gates of its own. The table lists them for the finance model, with thresholds that are illustrative and set per product.

| Gate | Measurement | Example threshold |
|---|---|---|
| Target gain | Exact match on the clean temporal test, paired against the prompt baseline | At least 3 points, with the cluster-bootstrap interval above zero |
| New companies | The entity-disjoint holdout, reported on its own | No worse than the prompt baseline |
| Retention | General capability suite of about 2,000 items, paired | No drop above 1 point on any area |
| Abstention | "Insufficient evidence" items | At least 90 percent correctly declined |
| Format | Output that parses into the five fields | At least 99.5 percent |
| Served artifact | The merged and quantized model actually deployed, on the development set | Within 0.5 points of the training checkpoint |

The gates are read as a set. A candidate ships only if every row passes, and a large gain on the target does not make up for a miss on retention or abstention, since each row protects a different use of the model. The thresholds are written before the results so that they cannot drift toward whatever the candidate happened to score. The target gate compares against the prompt baseline on the same questions. Pairing helps because a hard question is usually hard for both models, so the per-question differences vary less than either score does, and the interval on the gain is usually narrower than the $\pm 6.4$ points around a single score in the [time-split evaluation](#time-split-evaluation) section. Some thresholds translate directly into counts. On the 1,000 development questions, 0.5 points is five answers, and 99.5 percent parsing allows five malformed outputs in a thousand.

The served-artifact check exists because the deployed model is not the one that was evaluated. Merging adapters into the weights, quantizing to int8 or int4 as [serving](/tutorials/llm-fundamentals/inference-and-decoding/#quantization) does, and exporting to another runtime each change the numbers slightly, and digits are where small changes show. A release also keeps the previous model and adapter loadable, so rollback is a configuration change. It versions the corpus, tokenizer, split procedure, base model and recipe together, so any result can be rebuilt.

The decision itself comes from what the controlled tests showed. Each row maps a piece of evidence to the next step to try and to the result that would reject it.

| Evidence from controlled tests | Next step | Reject it if |
|---|---|---|
| Gold evidence repairs most failures | Retrieval with citations, or a long window for whole-document questions | The right passage still cannot be found reliably |
| Demonstrations repair the output | Supervised fine-tuning with task-specific examples, often with low-rank adapters | No gain over the prompt baseline on held-out companies and periods |
| Definitions repair many term errors across tasks | Domain-adaptive pretraining with replay, after a short decay test | Domain loss falls but slice losses and task scores do not improve |
| The tokenizer fragments common domain words | Tokenizer extension with mean-initialized rows, or a base with a better tokenizer | The token saving is small on held-out documents |
| The domain corpus is small and facts appear once | Synthetic question answering or synthetic continued pretraining | Gains vanish after decontaminating against the test set |
| Style complaints remain after fine-tuning | Preference tuning from the fine-tuned model | Exact match drops beyond the allowed threshold |
| No adapted open model meets the requirements | Mid-training on an owned run, or training from scratch | Total cost exceeds a stronger base plus retrieval |

The table is read from the top, and a row is tried only when the controlled tests point at it. The running example takes this path. The audit in the [failure diagnosis](#failure-diagnosis) section put 26 of 60 failures in the first row, so retrieval came first. Fourteen more fell in the second row, and the fine-tuning run in the [fine-tuning plan](#fine-tuning-plan) section raised exact match from the prompt baseline's 62.0 percent to 71.0 percent for about half a GPU-hour. Eight failures pointed at the third row, and the 20B-token run tested it. Its domain loss fell from 1.93 to 1.65 nats, but the digit slice rose from 2.60 to 2.70, wrong-period answers grew from 95 to 145, and exact match after the same fine-tuning fell to 68.0 percent. That meets the row's rejection condition, so the 568 GPU-hours are written off. The eight term errors go back to the one-line definitions that repaired them in the audit, added only for the terms a question involves. The candidate that goes to the gates is the fine-tuned model served with retrieval, with no continued pretraining.

The last row is rarely reached. [BloombergGPT](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.17564) is the published case of training a finance model from scratch. It trained a 50B-parameter model on 363B tokens of financial data mixed with 345B tokens of general data, roughly half and half. It remains a useful data point for what mixed-domain pretraining costs and buys, and its setup and results do not settle the choice for a new dataset or budget. Open bases trained on tens of trillions of tokens have appeared since then, such as Qwen3 at 36T. They already read most financial vocabulary well, so the usual path starts from such a base, puts facts behind retrieval, fine-tunes the behavior, and reaches for continued pretraining only when the audit shows the language itself is missing.
